\subsection{Tests on later dates}\label{sec:chronological_methods}

We evaluated later dates in two stages, to separate how well the surfaces transferred across dates from how well the simulator forecast complete option paths. The first stage used 58 capture dates through July 17, 2026. In each of 53 folds, we fitted the sector networks to the first $k$ dates and predicted the next date, for $k=5,\ldots,57$. This stage kept the original filters and capture-date labels, used the two surface inputs, and standardized the inputs using each training fold alone. An independent reconstruction of the saved row counts found that its 986{,}492 accepted observations had no crossed or missing asks and no missing or nonpositive displayed bid or ask sizes (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:chronological_data_audit}). Each prediction used the later date's stock price to compute moneyness. This stage therefore measured how well the surface predicted IV on a new date given the stock price. Stock forecasts and the IV updates entered only the second stage. The second stage fitted six sector networks once, using 1{,}173{,}538 observations from 68 trading sessions ending July 31 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:chronological_data_audit}). The architectures and training schedule matched the two-input sector model, with seed 42 and at most 2{,}000 epochs. We saved each network's final weights, ticker levels, and training-only standardization. The later sample contained 425{,}061 filtered IV observations across 23 sessions from August 4 through September 4. It served only as the final test, after the architectures, fitting windows, parameters, and seeds had been fixed.

Before fitting or selecting options, the second stage checked when each file had been downloaded (the capture audit). The download script recorded each retrieval time in UTC and stored the underlying's closing price with each option row. We accepted files retrieved after 20:00 UTC on the session date and before 13:30 UTC on the next trading session. This rule admitted overnight downloads of the previous close and excluded twenty repeat downloads taken during trading hours (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:chronological_data_audit}). We kept the latest eligible file per ticker and session and one row per exact option symbol. A two-sided quote needed a finite positive bid, an ask no lower than the bid, and positive displayed sizes. Surface fitting also required the original IV and moneyness ranges and a positive DTE recomputed from the expiration and the session date. Only files retrieved by the end of July 31 could enter training. Retrieval times record when each file was downloaded, not when each quote was set. Some quotes may therefore have been stale, and stock and option prices may come from slightly different moments, which affects both the IV errors and the dollar-price errors.

The forecast test used GS and LLY at successive forecast dates (origins) in August and early September. At each origin, we forecast one put and one call with 25 to 45 calendar DTE over planned horizons of one and five trading sessions. For each option type, we chose the expiration nearest 31 DTE and then the strike nearest $K/S=0.95$ for puts or $1.05$ for calls. Ties went to the earlier expiration and then by symbol, and the choice used only data available at the origin. We matched each chosen option at its exact endpoint session and recorded a missing endpoint as missing, without substituting another option or date. The data collection kept only certain maturity ranges each day, so none of these roughly one-month options was observed five sessions later (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_availability}). We kept their one-session evaluation and added a separate shorter-maturity cohort with 10 to 21 DTE, choosing the expiration nearest 14 DTE with the same strike and tie rules. We made this change, and recorded it in the experiment protocol, before looking at aggregate forecast errors. The five-session results therefore come from the shorter cohort. All five IV variants started from the option's observed IV at the origin. So that the fitted surface's starting level error would not enter the comparison, we rescaled the surface target once at the origin:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:origin_anchor}
\bar v^{*}_{a,t}=\sigma_{a,0,\mathrm{obs}}^2
\frac{\bar v_a(S_t,\tau_t)}{\bar v_a(S_0,\tau_0)},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{a,0,\mathrm{obs}}$ is the option's observed IV at the origin. The rescaled target started at the observed IV and kept the surface's relative changes with stock price and time to expiration. This rescaling applied only to the forecast test, and the IV ablation kept its fitted starting values. Frozen IV held the observed origin value, the direct surface followed $\bar v^{*}_{a,t}$, and the three evolving variants reverted toward $\bar v^{*}_{a,t}$ with the parameters used in the ablation. Each origin used 1{,}000 JumpHMM paths, with the same normal shocks for every IV variant. The starting hidden state was drawn from the stationary distribution of the model's transition matrix, with no jump episode in progress, so recent returns did not inform it in the original forecast. A separate 10{,}000-path pilot per ticker fixed the growth shift toward the 10\% annual prior and the one-session return standardization before the evaluation paths were drawn. A Gaussian random walk with zero log drift and the pilot's return standard deviation served as a stock benchmark. At each origin, the stock price and IV were updated to the latest observations while the fitted parameters stayed fixed. The test therefore evaluated forecasts issued at successive origins with the surfaces and model parameters fixed on July 31.

We scored the mean forecast option value against the later bid--ask midpoint, with mean absolute error (MAE) as the prespecified primary metric. We also scored the median forecast, to separate the choice of point summary from the IV comparison. Signed error, root mean squared error, and the share of forecast means inside the quoted spread served as additional price diagnostics. To score the whole forecast distribution, we used the empirical continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)~\cite{gneiting2007}. CRPS rewards forecasts concentrated near the observed value and penalizes probability placed far from it, and it is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forecast_crps}
\mathrm{CRPS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|P_j-y|
-\frac{1}{2N^2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|P_j-P_k|,
\end{equation}
where $P_1,\ldots,P_N$ are the simulated option values, $y$ is the observed midpoint, and smaller values indicate better forecasts. We also reported the coverage and width of central 90\% intervals. Scores were averaged across matched options within each ticker and origin, then across dates, and stock forecast scores were reported separately. A diagnostic repeated the IV updates along the observed stock path whenever every intervening session was available. Because it used the realized stock path, it measured how much of the option error came from the stock forecast. A second diagnostic repriced each option in the same tree with the IV reported at the endpoint, to isolate pricing discrepancies. All monetary scores are in dollars per share. Overlapping horizons and shared market moves made forecast dates and options dependent. The market evidence therefore rests on the origins, not on the number of simulated paths. The illustrated forecast was the earliest GS origin in the shorter-maturity cohort with both options observed at the five-session endpoint, a rule that ignored forecast errors.
