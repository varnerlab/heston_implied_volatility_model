\subsection{Tests on later dates}\label{sec:chronological_methods}

The tests on later dates trained surfaces on data up to a cutoff and scored them on dates after that cutoff, using the calibration files together with the later files. They ran as two separate tests, which we call stages. The first stage asked how well a surface predicted IV on a new date when that date's stock price was known. The second stage fitted surfaces through July 31, scored them on later IV quotes, and then used them to forecast option values, which also required forecasting the stock.

The first stage used 58 daily snapshots labeled April 14 through July 17, 2026, ordered by their folder labels, which kept the sessions in order, and filtered as the calibration data were. In each of 53 expanding-window folds, we refitted the sector networks from scratch on the first $k$ snapshots, with the calibration architecture and training schedule, and predicted IV on the next snapshot, for $k=5,\ldots,57$. The networks used the two surface inputs, moneyness and maturity, and each fold standardized them with its own training snapshots alone. Each prediction used the new date's stock price to compute moneyness, so this stage measured the surfaces alone, without a stock forecast or an IV update. We scored each fold by the root mean squared error (RMSE) of its IV predictions. A separate script rebuilt this data set from the raw files and reproduced its 986{,}492 filtered observations. It also confirmed that every observation passed the stricter two-sided-quote checks applied in the second stage (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:chronological_data_audit}).

The second stage fitted one set of surfaces on data through July 31 (the July-cutoff surfaces) and kept later sessions for a final test. The first stage used the files as saved. Some files, however, had been downloaded during the next day's trading hours, when the quotes were live but the stored price was still the previous close. The second stage therefore first checked when each file had been downloaded, a step we call the capture audit. The download script recorded each retrieval time in UTC along with the stored closing stock price. We accepted a file only if it was retrieved between 20:00 UTC on the date of the session whose close it stored and 13:30 UTC on the next trading day. For April through September, this window runs from the 4:00 p.m.\ New York close to the next 9:30 a.m.\ open. This rule admitted overnight downloads of a session's close and excluded twenty ticker files downloaded again during trading hours (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:chronological_data_audit}). We kept the latest eligible file per ticker and session and removed duplicate rows for the same option within a file. This stage also required two-sided quotes, with a finite positive bid, an ask no lower than the bid, and positive displayed sizes. Surface fitting kept the calibration filters on IV and moneyness and required a positive DTE computed from the expiration and the session date, because the DTE stored in some files counted from the download date instead. Retrieval times record when each file was downloaded, not when each quote was set. Some quotes may therefore have been stale, and stock and option prices may come from slightly different moments. These timing gaps may affect every IV error and dollar-price error in this study.

Training used the 68 captured trading sessions from April 13 through July 31. As a guard against look-ahead, it also excluded any file retrieved after the end of July 31 (UTC). The July 31 files were downloaded that evening, before this limit. We fitted six sector networks once on 1{,}173{,}538 observations (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:chronological_data_audit}). They used the two-input sector architecture and training schedule of the calibration comparison, with seed 42 and at most 2{,}000 epochs. We saved each network's final weights, ticker levels, and training-only standardization. The test sample contained 425{,}061 filtered IV observations from the 23 captured sessions between August 4 and September 4. No snapshots exist for August 3, the first session after the cutoff, or for August 20. We scored the July-cutoff surfaces on these observations by RMSE and by bias, the mean of predicted minus observed IV. The architecture, fitting window, fitted weights, and seed were all fixed before this test. After this test, we asked whether earnings reports explained its errors. Following a protocol written before the refit, we refitted the July-cutoff sector networks with and without the two earnings inputs, each under seeds 42, 43, and 44.

The forecast test compared simulated option values with later observations of GS and LLY options chosen on each forecast date. It issued forecasts on successive trading days, which we call forecast origins, from August 4 into early September. At each origin, it forecast one put and one call per ticker in each of two cohorts. The monthly cohort used options with 25 to 45 calendar DTE, taking the expiration nearest 31 DTE, and was scored one trading session later. The shorter-maturity cohort used options with 10 to 21 DTE, taking the expiration nearest 14 DTE, and was scored one and five sessions later. Within each cohort, we chose the strike nearest $K/S=0.95$ for puts or $1.05$ for calls. If two expirations were equally close to the target, we took the earlier one, and if two strikes were equally close, we took the lower one. The choice used only data available at the origin. We matched each chosen option at its exact endpoint session and recorded a missing endpoint as missing, without substituting another option or date. The monthly cohort was our original design for both the one- and five-session horizons. The downloaded files, however, kept only the expirations nearest the 2-, 7-, 14-, 30-, and 45-day targets. Five sessions after selection, a monthly option had about 24 days left, between the 14- and 30-day targets. Other expirations lay closer to both targets, so this one was no longer saved. None of these options was therefore observed at five sessions (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_availability}). We therefore added the shorter-maturity cohort. Its options, chosen near the 14-day target, had about a week left five sessions later and were then nearest the 7-day target, so most of them stayed in the data. We made this change, and recorded it in the written analysis protocol for this test, before looking at aggregate forecast errors.

At each origin, the forecast used the latest observed stock price and option IVs, while the July-cutoff surfaces, the JumpHMM parameters fitted on 2014--2024 prices, and the IV-update constants stayed fixed. We drew 1{,}000 JumpHMM stock paths per origin, with a fixed seed for each origin, and every IV variant used the same shocks. The starting hidden state was drawn from the stationary distribution of the model's transition matrix, with no jump episode in progress, so recent returns did not inform it. A separate 10{,}000-path pilot simulation for each ticker, reused at every origin, fixed the growth shift for the 10\% target and the return mean and standard deviation before the evaluation paths were drawn. As a stock-only benchmark, a Gaussian random walk with zero log drift and the pilot's return standard deviation also forecast each endpoint stock price. All five IV variants started from the IV that Alpaca reported for the option at the origin. To keep the fitted surface's error in the starting IV level out of the comparison, we rescaled the surface target once at the origin. The rescaled target was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:origin_anchor}
\bar v^{*}_{a,t}=\sigma_{a,0,\mathrm{obs}}^2
\frac{\bar v_{a,t}}{\bar v_{a,0}},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{a,0,\mathrm{obs}}$ is the option's observed IV at the origin and $\bar v_{a,t}$ is the target in~\eqref{eq:theta} along each simulated path. This target started at the square of the observed IV and kept the surface's relative changes with stock price and time to expiration. The rescaling applied only to the forecast test, and the IV ablation kept its fitted starting values. Frozen IV held the observed origin value, the direct surface followed $\bar v^{*}_{a,t}$, and the three updating variants reverted toward $\bar v^{*}_{a,t}$ with the IV-ablation parameters.

We scored the mean forecast option value against the later bid--ask midpoint, with mean absolute error (MAE) as the primary metric, fixed before the test. To check that conclusions did not depend on the choice of point summary, we also scored the median forecast. Signed error, RMSE, and the share of forecast means inside the quoted spread served as additional price diagnostics. To score the whole forecast distribution, we used the empirical continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)~\cite{gneiting2007}. The CRPS rewards forecasts concentrated near the observed value and penalizes probability placed far from it, and it is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forecast_crps}
\mathrm{CRPS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|P_j-y|
-\frac{1}{2N^2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}|P_j-P_l|,
\end{equation}
where $P_1,\ldots,P_N$ are the option values at the endpoint on the $N=N_p$ simulated paths, $y$ is the observed midpoint, and smaller values indicate better forecasts. We also reported how often the central 90\% interval of the simulated values contained the observed midpoint (coverage) and how wide that interval was. All monetary scores are in dollars per share. For each ticker, cohort, and horizon, we averaged the scores of the options matched at the endpoint on each origin, the put, the call, or both, and then averaged across origins. Overlapping horizons and shared market moves made the origins dependent, so the effective number of independent observations was smaller than the number of origins, and adding simulated paths could not increase it.

Two diagnostics located the sources of forecast error. The first repeated the IV updates along the realized stock path whenever every intervening session was observed. It standardized the realized returns with the pilot constants and still drew the independent shocks, so its option errors excluded the error of the stock forecast. The second priced each option at its endpoint in the 201-step LR tree, using the observed endpoint stock price and the IV that Alpaca reported at the endpoint. It then compared this price with the observed midpoint. Its errors measure how far the tree, given the reported IV, departs from the quoted price. We also scored the stock forecasts of the 1{,}000 JumpHMM paths and of the random walk at each endpoint. For the example forecast figure, we chose the earliest GS origin in the shorter-maturity cohort with both options observed at the five-session endpoint, a rule that did not depend on forecast errors.
