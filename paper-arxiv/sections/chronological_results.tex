To test forecasts of later option prices, we first extended the surface evaluation beyond the original April--May window and observed uneven accuracy on later dates (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:walk_forward_extended} and~\ref{tab:chronological_surface}). In the expanding-window test, two-input sector networks predicted 53 successive held-out capture dates through July 17. The median test RMSE was 9.92 volatility percentage points, but individual folds ranged from 7.68 to 19.25 points (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:walk_forward_extended}). We then fitted one set of sector surfaces on data through July 31 (the July-cutoff surfaces) and scored them on the 23 later sessions from August 4 through September 4. Pooled RMSE rose from 10.79 points in training to 14.75 points on the held-out sessions (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:chronological_surface}). The fitting window, capture audit, and evaluation dates differed from those of the earlier folds, so the two sets of errors could not be compared directly. For GS, the held-out RMSE was 6.30 points, and the surface overpredicted IV by 0.88 points on average. For LLY, the held-out RMSE was 9.96 points, and the average overprediction of 4.61 points was more than five times the GS bias (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:chronological_surface}). Even with the larger data set, a fixed surface level still lagged later market IV. This lag motivated a separate forecast comparison in which all IV variants started from the observed IV at the forecast origin and differed only in how they evolved afterward.

We next compared option forecasts with later observations of the same options and found nearly identical errors under the full factor and frozen IV (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_main}). None of the 36 eligible roughly one-month options per ticker was observed again five sessions later, so these options were scored after one session only (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_availability}). That comparison kept 34 quotes across 17 origins per ticker. After one session, the full factor's MAE was \$3.79 per share for GS and \$6.08 for LLY, compared with \$3.80 and \$6.11 under frozen IV (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_one_session}). Five-session outcomes came from the shorter-maturity cohort, which supplied 34 GS and 30 LLY quotes across 17 origins per ticker. Coupling again reduced the MAE of the forecast mean only slightly. For GS, the MAE fell from \$4.21 under frozen IV to \$4.19, and for LLY it fell from \$11.62 to \$11.59. The full factor had lower date-level MAE than frozen IV on six of seventeen GS origins and nine of seventeen LLY origins (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_main}). These small average reductions were therefore not consistent across dates. Scoring the forecast median instead of the mean lowered the GS full-factor MAE to \$2.48 and gave an LLY MAE of \$11.79. Both values were again close to those under frozen IV (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:option_point_summary}). The full factor's CRPS was also close to that of frozen IV, at 2.01 versus 2.02 for GS and 8.93 versus 8.99 for LLY (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_main}). Under the full factor, date-weighted coverage of the central 90\% intervals was 100\% for GS but 67.6\% for LLY. The LLY coverage was 22 percentage points below the nominal level. At the earliest eligible GS origin, the full-factor and frozen-IV mean forecasts nearly coincided, while the observed call value fell below both (Fig.~\ref{fig:chronological_forecast}).

To find where these errors came from, we replaced the simulated stock paths with the observed paths and found that stock-forecast error accounted for most of the option-price error (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_conditional}). This substitution was possible for thirteen five-session origins per ticker, on which every intervening stock price was observed. These origins gave 26 matched GS quotes and 24 LLY quotes. In these cases, the substitution cut the full-factor MAE 3.8-fold for GS, from \$4.62 to \$1.23 per share. For LLY, it cut the MAE 5.4-fold, from \$10.61 to \$1.95 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_conditional}). Because the observed path was unknown at the origin, the substitution served only as a diagnostic that located the source of the error. Within the diagnostic, frozen IV had lower point errors than the full factor, at \$1.07 for GS and \$1.74 for LLY. The uncoupled stochastic factor had lower CRPS than the full factor for both tickers. For the stock itself, JumpHMM also gave no point-forecast advantage over a Gaussian random walk. Scored on its mean forecast, its five-session stock MAE was \$16.85 for GS and \$49.83 for LLY, compared with \$16.17 and \$48.64 for the random walk (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_stock}). A second diagnostic repriced each shorter-maturity option at its endpoint with its reported IV, which reduced five-session option MAE to \$0.14 for GS and \$0.34 for LLY. Every repriced value lay within its observed bid--ask interval (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_repricing}). Refining the tree on sampled paths changed prices by at most \$0.0026 per share across the two cohorts, below the forecast errors (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_numerical_checks}). Stock-forecast error therefore dominated the option-price misses, and smaller errors remained in the IV and pricing inputs. No IV variant was reliably better than the others.
