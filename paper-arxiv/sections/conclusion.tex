\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

In this study, we built a simulator that values American options along stock paths from a separately chosen stock model, here the jump hidden Markov model JumpHMM, by updating each option's IV on every simulated date. Each option's implied variance moved partway each day toward the prediction of a neural surface fitted to past quotes, following the mean-reverting variance equation of the Heston model with random shocks, and a binomial tree converted the resulting IV into the option's price. More flexible surfaces, fitted separately to smaller groups of tickers, matched the fitting data more closely, but their accuracy on later dates was uneven. Repricing identical stock paths under different IV updates changed option values before expiration and the outcome of closing positions early, while payoffs at expiration stayed the same. In forecasts of later option prices, the IV update with random shocks linked to stock returns showed no consistent advantage over holding IV fixed. Repeating the forecasts with the stock paths that actually occurred, information not available when forecasting, reduced option errors, which motivated a separate test of the stock forecasts. In stock forecasts for 2025 and for August and September 2026, JumpHMM and two simpler stock models predicted prices about as accurately as assuming no change. The simpler model whose volatility adapted to recent returns improved the predicted range of stock prices for LLY in both periods but not for GS, and tests on 2019--2024 data found no benefit from predicting direction with recent stock and market returns. These findings support the framework as a reproducible way to compare IV and stock assumptions, while limiting claims about its forecasting accuracy. JumpHMM remains the example stock model, and we did not replace it with either simpler model. Saved observations, fitted models, simulation outputs, and scoring code make the comparisons reviewable. Incomplete histories of one-month options, overlapping evaluation dates, the small set of models compared, and remaining price inconsistencies across strikes limit broader use.
