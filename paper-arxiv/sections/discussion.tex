\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

In this study, we developed a framework for examining how IV assumptions affect American-option values while keeping a chosen stock-return model, here JumpHMM, fixed. Repricing identical stock paths under five ways of setting IV, namely holding it fixed at its starting value (frozen IV), setting it each day to the fitted surface's prediction, moving it partway toward that prediction each day, adding random shocks to that movement, and linking those shocks to stock returns, changed option values before expiration and the outcome of closing short positions early, while starting values and payoffs at expiration agreed exactly, which made the experiment a controlled comparison of the IV assumptions. The pattern of those changes followed from how each option's implied variance was updated in~\eqref{eq:euler}. With $\kappa=15$ and daily steps, each trading day closed about 6\% of the gap between an option's implied variance and the variance predicted by the fitted surface, so half of any gap remained after about eleven trading days. The version without random shocks therefore lagged behind the moving surface prediction, and after ten trading days its P\&L differences from setting IV each day directly to the surface prediction were at least three quarters as large as those of frozen IV (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). The random shocks averaged zero, and adding them raised the GS put's average absolute price difference from prices with IV set directly to the surface prediction from \$0.42 without shocks to \$1.75 with them while changing its average P\&L difference by \$0.06, which explains why large price changes on individual paths left average P\&L nearly unchanged. Linking the shocks to stock returns with a negative coefficient raised IV after the stock fell and lowered it after the stock rose, the same direction as the leverage effect observed in equity data~\cite{cont2001}. It therefore raised put values on the falling-stock paths where short puts already lost money and lowered call values on the rising-stock paths where short calls lost money, which increased the average loss in the worst 5\% of outcomes (expected shortfall) for short puts and reduced it for short calls for both GS and LLY.

The forecast tests asked a separate question, whether the model predicted later market prices, and there the full IV update, with its parameters held fixed, reduced the error of the average option forecast only slightly relative to frozen IV, with no consistent advantage across dates or when forecasts were summarized by the median instead of the mean, while its central 90\% price ranges contained every observed GS price but only about two thirds of LLY prices (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_main}). The slow adjustment also limited what the IV update could add over five trading days. Every version started from the IV observed on the forecast date, with each option's surface prediction rescaled to equal that IV on that date, so gaps opened only as stock moves and the passage of time changed the prediction. The update closed only about a quarter of any such gap in five days, and the changing prediction and the random shocks raised IV on some paths and lowered it on others. Averaged across paths, the updated IV therefore stayed within 0.3 volatility points of its starting value, which frozen IV kept throughout, although individual paths moved about 2.5 points (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}), so the two average option forecasts could not differ materially. An update with fixed parameters that pulls IV toward a fixed surface carries no information about future IV beyond how the surface prediction changes with moneyness and time to expiration, whereas observed IV surfaces shift in level, skew, and curvature over time~\cite{cont2002}, so a better forecast would have required an input that tracked the IV level. The framework's contribution was the ability to compare these assumptions while holding the options, stock paths, and pricing calculation fixed. That control made the measured changes and their limits interpretable, and it kept identical payoffs at expiration from being mistaken for evidence that the simulated IV paths before expiration were realistic. Agreement within the simulator is not agreement with the market, which the comparisons with later option and stock prices tested separately.

The stock forecast tests showed why this distinction mattered for forward use. Repeating the option forecasts with the stock paths that actually occurred cut option errors 3.8- to 5.4-fold on the same options and dates, which identified the stock forecast as the main source of option forecast error, although that information would not have been available when a forecast was made. JumpHMM's median stock forecasts nearly matched a forecast of no price change because of its drift. We imposed an average log return of 10\% a year on JumpHMM, about 0.2\% over five trading days, whereas the simulated five-day log return had a standard deviation near 4\%, about twenty times larger. The median simulated price therefore rose by an average of only 0.16--0.27\% from the starting price (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}), and its errors nearly matched those of the no-change forecast. Expected returns are small relative to short-horizon return variation, and even a constant expected return can be estimated precisely only from long spans of data~\cite{merton1980}. Regressions that predict equity returns have also rarely beaten the historical average out of sample~\cite{welch2008}, which is consistent with our finding that the simple model predicting direction from recent stock and market returns forecast 2019--2024 prices best with its predicted drift set to zero. These findings concern the specified models and inputs rather than the general possibility of predicting returns. Starting JumpHMM in the hidden state implied by recent returns did not materially improve five-day forecasts either, since the model's switching between states erased the influence of the starting state within a few days.

The simple model that scaled its forecasts by recent volatility helped LLY but not GS, because volatility, unlike direction, persists enough to be forecast from recent returns~\cite{cont2001,andersen1998} and LLY's volatility stayed above JumpHMM's level in both test periods. Because each JumpHMM forecast started from the model's long-run state probabilities, the width of its forecasts stayed at the volatility of its 2014--2024 fit, whereas the standard deviation of LLY's daily returns was 1.5 times that level in 2025 and 1.3 times in August and September 2026 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). The adaptive model's volatility, an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) of recent squared returns, tracked those levels and widened LLY's forecast ranges toward the observed spread of outcomes. That improved LLY's continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), which rewards forecast distributions concentrated near the observed outcome, in both periods, although its 90\% ranges still contained fewer than 90\% of the 2025 outcomes. GS's 2025 daily volatility was within 10\% of the JumpHMM level, so adapting it changed the score little. In August and September 2026, the standard deviation of GS's daily returns was 1.40\%, below both the 1.80\% JumpHMM level and the adaptive estimate, which April--July returns had raised to an average of 2.26\%. The 90\% ranges of both models already contained every 2026 GS outcome, so the adaptive model's wider ranges made its forecasts less precise without making them more reliable, a trade that CRPS penalizes~\cite{gneiting2007calibration}. The evidence therefore supported widening forecast ranges for a ticker whose recent volatility exceeded JumpHMM's level, not replacing the stock model or using its median forecasts as predictions of direction, and we retained JumpHMM as the example stock model and reported the two simpler models as comparisons.

Separating the stock model from the IV model also left requirements that forecast comparisons could not address. A binomial tree handles early exercise for each option separately but does not make prices consistent across strikes, and the GS prices that violated no-arbitrage conditions across strikes showed this limitation even when IV was set directly from the fitted surface (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:strike_audit}). For a strategy that holds several options, those inconsistencies matter because decisions depend on relative prices as well as the value of each position, and surface methods that impose no-arbitrage constraints offer one way to meet that requirement before simulated prices are used in place of market data~\cite{cont2023}. Fitting the surfaces posed a second problem. The neural surfaces fitted IV more closely than the parametric surface across all observations, yet on May 11, one of the dates used for fitting, they overpredicted near-the-money IV for MSFT and LLY, and the surface fitted on 68 trading days through July 31 showed a related error on later dates. Daily errors showed that LLY's overprediction on later dates began after a scheduled earnings report. The surface fitted through July underpredicted LLY's IV by 17.90 volatility points on August 4, the trading day before the August 5 report, and overpredicted it by 2.04 to 8.07 points on every observed day from August 6 through September 4 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}), the rise before and fall after an anticipated announcement that option prices have long shown~\cite{patell1979,dubinsky2019}. Each ticker's fitted IV level averaged over the fitting period, so it could not represent IV both before and after a report, whereas GS, whose July 14 report lay inside the fitting period and whose next report came after the test period, had daily errors between 3.07 points below and 3.59 points above observed IV on later dates. Averaged over the five trading days before and after each ticker's latest report, the surface's error shifted by only 3.4 points for GS, compared with 16.4 points for LLY (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). The May 11 errors fit the same explanation, since MSFT and LLY, the two tickers whose IV the surfaces overpredicted, reported on April 29 and April 30, after ten of the fifteen fitting days (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:smile_date_bias}). Adding the time to scheduled earnings reports as a surface input reduced error when surfaces fitted on earlier April dates were tested on April 23 and 24 (Table~\ref{tab:earnings_holdout}), and refitting the July surfaces with the same inputs cut LLY's overprediction after its report from 6.17 to 3.85 points, each averaged across three training seeds (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:earnings_refit}). That reduction fell short of the halving we had set in advance as support, so report timing explained part of LLY's error but not all of it, which is consistent with each sector network sharing one earnings response among tickers whose IV moved by different amounts around their reports. In the forecast tests, rescaling the surface to the IV observed on each forecast date removed the starting level error, but it was an explicit change to the starting values and did not make prices consistent across options. Pricing each option with the IV that Alpaca, the data vendor, reported on the forecast's end date reduced errors further, although it used information from that later date, and agreement with Alpaca's indicative bid and ask did not establish agreement with exchange quotes or show that Alpaca's Black--Scholes IV always reproduces prices in an American binomial tree. Accurate IV surfaces, accurate stock forecasts, accurate option prices, and prices consistent across strikes therefore remained separate requirements, and meeting one did not establish the others or make the framework a joint pricing model of the stock and its volatility.

The evidence is limited by the data source, the observation windows, and the scope of model selection. All option observations came from Alpaca's free indicative feed, whose quotes are derived from exchange quotes rather than copied from them, which supported comparisons with the captured vendor data but not claims about prices at which trades could execute, and the download times did not establish that stock and option prices were recorded at the same moment. The first surface comparisons used fifteen daily snapshots, and the later-date tests trained each network with a single random seed. The option forecast test covered 23 trading days, and the 2025 test covered stock forecasts only. Overlapping forecast periods and shared market moves reduced the independent information in both tests, which more simulated paths could not restore. Because the data collection kept only certain expiration ranges each day, the one-month options first selected for the forecast tests had no quotes five trading days later, so the shorter-dated options added afterward did not validate the one-month simulation examples. We proposed the two simpler stock models after seeing the August and September 2026 forecast misses, although earlier data chose their settings, and the few return inputs and settings we tested support no general claim that one model is better. The earnings explanation rested on a few report dates for three tickers, and the refit that tested it used one earnings calendar, three training seeds, and a single test period. JumpHMM's fitted parameters and the parameters of the IV update, including its link to stock returns, stayed fixed, and two proposed extensions that would let the IV level depend on JumpHMM's hidden state and its visits to the states that produce extreme returns were switched off. Capping JumpHMM's heavy-tailed daily returns at ten times each state's fitted return scale gave simulated prices a finite mean and variance, but neither that cap nor the wider cap tested for sensitivity was shown to match the largest real stock moves. The check across strikes used a finite set of strikes and did not test consistency across expiration dates or over time, and valuation omitted dividends, execution costs, margin, premium financing, hedging, and early assignment before the end of the horizon. These limits restrict the present use of the framework to controlled modeling comparisons and the specific forecasting evidence reported here. Complete daily histories of the same options, collected across different market conditions, would allow the forecast tests to be repeated on one-month options and the switched-off extensions to be evaluated, and that evaluation should precede conclusions about practical trading use.
