\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

How IV was set mattered before expiration, even when the stock paths stayed the same. We held a chosen stock-return model fixed, here the jump hidden Markov model JumpHMM, and repriced American options along identical stock paths under five ways of setting IV. One held IV fixed at its starting value (frozen IV), and another set IV each day to the fitted surface's prediction, which we call the target. The other three moved IV partway toward that target each day, either without random shocks, with independent random shocks, or with random shocks linked to stock returns (the full IV update). The choice changed option values and the outcome of closing short positions early, whereas starting values and payoffs at expiration agreed exactly. This made the experiment a controlled comparison of the IV assumptions. These differences matter because option positions are managed on every date before expiration, and an analysis of payoffs at expiration alone would miss them. The mechanics of the IV update in~\eqref{eq:euler} explain these differences. The update used a mean-reversion rate of $\kappa=15$ per year and daily steps. Each trading day therefore closed about 6\% of the gap between an option's implied variance (the square of its IV) and the variance predicted by the fitted surface. Half of any gap remained after about eleven trading days, so the version without random shocks lagged behind a target that moved with the stock price and the time to expiration. We measured each version's prices and profit and loss (P\&L) against the version that set IV directly to the target. After ten trading days, the P\&L differences for the version without shocks were at least three quarters as large as those for frozen IV (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). Over ten days, that version therefore stayed closer to frozen IV than to the target. Adding random shocks had a different effect, because the shocks averaged zero. For the GS put, the average absolute difference from the price with IV set directly to the target rose from \$0.42 without shocks to \$1.75 with independent shocks. The average P\&L difference, however, changed by only \$0.06 between these two versions. Price changes on individual paths were large, but they canceled across paths and left average P\&L nearly unchanged. Linking the shocks to stock returns with a negative coefficient raised IV after the stock fell and lowered it after the stock rose. Stock volatility tends to move the same way, rising after prices fall, a pattern known as the leverage effect~\cite{cont2001}. This link raised put values on falling-stock paths, where short puts already lost money, and lowered call values on rising-stock paths, where short calls lost money. Compared with independent shocks, the link therefore increased the average loss in the worst 5\% of outcomes (expected shortfall) for short puts on both GS and LLY and reduced it for short calls.

The forecast tests asked whether the simulator also predicted later market prices of the same options. With its parameters held fixed, the full IV update reduced the mean absolute error of the mean option forecast only slightly relative to frozen IV (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_main}). This small advantage was not consistent across dates, and it did not persist when forecasts were summarized by the median instead of the mean (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_main}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:option_point_summary}). The full update's central 90\% price ranges contained every observed GS price but only about two thirds of LLY prices. The slow adjustment toward the target limited what the IV update could add over five trading days. All five versions started from the IV observed on the forecast date. Each option's target was rescaled to equal that IV on that date, so gaps opened only as stock moves and the passage of time changed the target. The update closed only about a quarter of any such gap in five days. The changing target and the random shocks also raised IV on some paths and lowered it on others. Averaged across paths, the updated IV therefore stayed within 0.3 volatility points of its starting value, although individual paths moved about 2.5 points (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). Frozen IV kept the starting value throughout, so the two average option forecasts stayed close. An update with fixed parameters that pulls IV toward a fixed surface carries no information about future IV beyond how the surface's prediction changes with moneyness and time to expiration. Observed IV surfaces, by contrast, shift over time. Index-option surfaces, for example, move in level, skew, and curvature~\cite{cont2002}. A better forecast would likely need an input that tracked the IV level. Without one, the IV update serves to compare IV assumptions on identical stock paths rather than to forecast IV.

Those comparisons pointed to the stock forecast as the main source of option forecast error. Repeating the option forecasts with the stock paths that actually occurred cut option errors 3.8- to 5.4-fold on the same options and dates (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_conditional}). The median stock forecasts from JumpHMM nearly matched a forecast of no price change (Table~\ref{tab:stock_validation}), because the drift we imposed on JumpHMM was small. That drift was an average log return of 10\% a year, about 0.2\% over five trading days. The simulated five-day log return had a standard deviation near 4\%, about twenty times larger. The median simulated price therefore rose by an average of only 0.16--0.27\% from the starting price (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}), so its errors nearly matched those of the no-change forecast. Expected returns are small relative to short-horizon return variation, and even a constant expected return can be estimated precisely only from long spans of data~\cite{merton1980}. Regressions that predict the stock market's excess return have also rarely beaten its historical average out of sample~\cite{welch2008}. We also compared JumpHMM with two simpler stock models. One of them, the direction-predicting model, forecast drift from recent stock and market returns, and it behaved the same way. Tuned on 2019--2024 data, it forecast best with its drift set to zero (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:stock_selection}). Starting JumpHMM in the hidden state implied by recent returns did not materially improve five-day forecasts either (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:stock_initialization}). The model's switching between states largely erased the influence of the starting state within a few days.

The other simpler model, the adaptive-volatility model, scaled its forecast ranges by recent volatility, estimated as an exponentially weighted moving average of recent squared returns. It helped LLY but not GS (Table~\ref{tab:stock_validation}). Volatility, unlike direction, persists enough to be forecast from recent returns~\cite{cont2001,andersen1998}. In both test periods, LLY's volatility also stayed above JumpHMM's level. Each JumpHMM forecast started from the model's long-run state probabilities, so the width of its forecasts stayed at the volatility of its 2014--2024 fit. The standard deviation of LLY's daily returns was 1.5 times that level in 2025 and 1.3 times in August and September 2026 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). The adaptive-volatility estimate tracked those levels and widened LLY's forecast ranges toward the observed spread of outcomes. The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) rewards forecast distributions concentrated near the observed outcome. The wider ranges improved LLY's CRPS in both periods, because LLY's outcomes spread more widely than JumpHMM's ranges allowed. Even so, the adaptive-volatility model's 90\% ranges contained fewer than 90\% of LLY's 2025 outcomes (Table~\ref{tab:stock_validation}). In 2025, GS's daily volatility was within 10\% of the JumpHMM level, so the adaptive-volatility model changed GS's score little (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). In August and September 2026, the standard deviation of GS's daily returns was 1.40\% (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). This was below both the 1.80\% JumpHMM level and the adaptive-volatility estimate, which April--July returns had raised to an average of 2.26\%. The 90\% ranges of both models already contained every 2026 GS outcome (Table~\ref{tab:stock_validation}). The adaptive-volatility model's wider ranges therefore made its forecasts less precise without making them more reliable, and the CRPS penalizes that trade~\cite{gneiting2007calibration}. The two tickers therefore favor widening JumpHMM's forecast ranges only when a ticker's volatility stays above the model's level, as LLY's did. Recent volatility alone was not a reliable signal, because GS's April--July returns overstated its volatility in August and September. Neither the adaptive-volatility model nor the direction-predicting model beat JumpHMM consistently across tickers and periods, so we kept JumpHMM as the example stock model and reported both as comparisons.

Two further problems concern the simulated prices themselves. The first was that the binomial tree prices each option on its own, so nothing forces prices to be consistent across strikes. Some GS prices violated no-arbitrage conditions across strikes, even when IV was set directly from the fitted surface (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:strike_audit}). For a strategy that holds several options, those inconsistencies matter because decisions depend on relative prices as well as the value of each position. Methods that simulate IV surfaces under no-arbitrage constraints offer a starting point for removing them~\cite{cont2023}. Those constraints apply to European options, so American prices from the tree would still need their own checks before simulated prices replace market data. The second problem lay in the fitted surfaces, whose errors on some dates traced partly to scheduled earnings reports. The neural surfaces, which allow more flexible shapes, fitted IV more closely than the parametric surface across all observations (Table~\ref{tab:three_way}). Yet on May 11, one of the dates used for fitting, they overpredicted near-the-money IV for MSFT and LLY. The surface fitted on 68 trading days through July 31 made a related error on later dates. For LLY, its daily errors showed that the overprediction began after a scheduled earnings report. The surface underpredicted LLY's IV by 17.90 volatility points on August 4, the trading day before the August 5 report. It then overpredicted LLY's IV by 2.04 to 8.07 points on every observed day from August 6 through September 4 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). Option prices have long shown this pattern, with IV rising before an anticipated announcement and falling after it~\cite{patell1979,dubinsky2019}. Because each ticker's fitted IV level averaged over the fitting period, one level could not represent IV both before and after a report. For GS, the July 14 report lay inside the fitting period, and the next report came after the test period. The GS errors on later dates ranged from 3.07 points below to 3.59 points above observed IV. Averaged over the five observed sessions before and after each ticker's latest report, the surface's error shifted by 3.36 points for GS, compared with 16.45 points for LLY (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). The May 11 errors fit the same explanation. The two tickers whose IV the surfaces overpredicted, MSFT and LLY, reported on April 29 and April 30, after ten of the fifteen fitting days (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:smile_date_bias}). Adding the time to scheduled earnings reports as a surface input reduced error when surfaces fitted on earlier April dates were tested on April 23 and 24 (Table~\ref{tab:earnings_holdout}). Refitting the July surfaces with these earnings inputs cut LLY's overprediction after its report from 6.17 to 3.85 points, with both values averaged across three training seeds (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:earnings_refit}). That reduction fell short of the halving that we had set in advance as the criterion for supporting the earnings explanation. Report timing therefore explained part of LLY's error but not all of it. The remainder is consistent with each sector's surface applying one earnings response to all its tickers, whose IV moved by different amounts around their reports. Both problems also shaped the forecast tests. Rescaling the target to the IV observed on each forecast date removed the error in the starting IV level but left the inconsistencies across strikes in place. Pricing each option with the IV that Alpaca, the data vendor, reported on the forecast's end date reduced errors more than using the realized stock paths did. That check, however, used information from the later date (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_repricing}). The tests therefore separated four requirements that a synthetic option market must meet: accurate IV surfaces, accurate stock forecasts, a pricing model that matches market prices given the right IV, and prices consistent across strikes.

The data were the main limitation. All option quotes came from Alpaca's free indicative feed, which is derived from exchange quotes. Our price errors therefore measured agreement with Alpaca's quotes, not with prices at which trades could execute. The download times also did not tie stock and option prices to the same moment. The observation windows were short. The surface comparisons used fifteen daily snapshots, and the option forecasts covered 23 trading days. Most networks in the tests on later dates were also trained with a single random seed. Overlapping forecast periods and shared market moves further reduced the independent information in these tests. Our daily collection saved only expirations near a fixed ladder of maturities, so the one-month options we first selected had no quotes five trading days later (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_availability}). The shorter-dated options that replaced them could not validate the one-month GS and LLY examples. Several conclusions rest on thin evidence. We proposed the adaptive-volatility and direction-predicting models after seeing JumpHMM's August and September forecast misses. The earnings explanation rests on a few reports for three tickers. Some modeling choices also went unchecked. We did not test the cap we placed on JumpHMM's daily returns against the largest real stock moves. The IV update's parameters were set by assumption rather than estimated from data. Two optional features in the code, which would let the IV level depend on JumpHMM's current hidden state and on its visits to extreme-return states, stayed switched off. The present results therefore support controlled comparisons of IV assumptions and the specific forecasts reported here, not trading use. Complete daily histories of the same options, collected across different market conditions, would allow a repeat of the forecast tests on one-month options and a test of the switched-off features.
