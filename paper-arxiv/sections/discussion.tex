\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

In this study, we developed a framework for examining how IV assumptions affect American-option values while keeping a chosen stock-return model, here JumpHMM, fixed. We repriced identical stock paths under five ways of setting IV. These held IV fixed at its starting value (frozen IV), set it each day to the fitted surface's prediction, moved it partway toward that prediction each day, added random shocks to that movement, or linked those shocks to stock returns. The choice changed option values before expiration and the outcome of closing short positions early. Starting values and payoffs at expiration agreed exactly, so the experiment was a controlled comparison of the IV assumptions. The pattern of those changes followed from how each option's implied variance was updated in~\eqref{eq:euler}. With $\kappa=15$ and daily steps, each trading day closed about 6\% of the gap between an option's implied variance and the variance predicted by the fitted surface. Half of any gap therefore remained after about eleven trading days. The version without random shocks lagged behind the moving surface prediction as a result. After ten trading days, its P\&L differences from setting IV each day directly to the surface prediction were at least three quarters as large as those of frozen IV (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). The random shocks averaged zero. For the GS put, adding them raised the average absolute price difference from prices with IV set directly to the surface prediction from \$0.42 to \$1.75, while the average P\&L difference changed by \$0.06. Price changes on individual paths were therefore large, but they canceled across paths and left average P\&L nearly unchanged. Linking the shocks to stock returns with a negative coefficient raised IV after the stock fell and lowered it after the stock rose. Equity data show the same direction of change, known as the leverage effect~\cite{cont2001}. This link raised put values on falling-stock paths, where short puts already lost money. It lowered call values on rising-stock paths, where short calls lost money. For both GS and LLY, it therefore increased the average loss in the worst 5\% of outcomes (expected shortfall) for short puts and reduced it for short calls.

The forecast tests asked a separate question, whether the model predicted later market prices. With its parameters held fixed, the full IV update reduced the error of the average option forecast only slightly relative to frozen IV (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_main}). The advantage was not consistent across dates or when forecasts were summarized by the median instead of the mean (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_main}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:option_point_summary}). The update's central 90\% price ranges contained every observed GS price but only about two thirds of LLY prices. The slow adjustment also limited what the IV update could add over five trading days. Every version started from the IV observed on the forecast date. Each option's surface prediction was rescaled to equal that IV on that date, so gaps opened only as stock moves and the passage of time changed the prediction. The update closed only about a quarter of any such gap in five days. The changing prediction and the random shocks also raised IV on some paths and lowered it on others. Averaged across paths, the updated IV therefore stayed within 0.3 volatility points of its starting value, although individual paths moved about 2.5 points (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). Frozen IV kept the starting value throughout, so the two average option forecasts could not differ materially. An update with fixed parameters that pulls IV toward a fixed surface carries no information about future IV beyond how the surface prediction changes with moneyness and time to expiration. Observed IV surfaces, by contrast, shift in level, skew, and curvature over time~\cite{cont2002}. A better forecast would have required an input that tracked the IV level. The simulator's contribution was to compare these IV assumptions on the same options, stock paths, and pricing calculation. Whether any of them matched the market was a separate question, which only the comparisons with later option and stock prices could answer.

Those comparisons pointed to the stock forecast. Repeating the option forecasts with the stock paths that actually occurred cut option errors 3.8- to 5.4-fold on the same options and dates (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_conditional}), so the stock forecast was the main source of option forecast error. JumpHMM's median stock forecasts nearly matched a forecast of no price change because of its drift (Table~\ref{tab:stock_validation}). We imposed an average log return of 10\% a year on JumpHMM, about 0.2\% over five trading days. The simulated five-day log return had a standard deviation near 4\%, about twenty times larger. The median simulated price therefore rose by an average of only 0.16--0.27\% from the starting price (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}), so its errors nearly matched those of the no-change forecast. Expected returns are small relative to short-horizon return variation, and even a constant expected return can be estimated precisely only from long spans of data~\cite{merton1980}. Regressions that predict equity returns have also rarely beaten the historical average out of sample~\cite{welch2008}. Our direction-predicting model, which forecast drift from recent stock and market returns, behaved the same way. It forecast 2019--2024 prices best with its drift set to zero (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:stock_selection}). Starting JumpHMM in the hidden state implied by recent returns did not materially improve five-day forecasts either (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:stock_initialization}). The model's switching between states erased the influence of the starting state within a few days.

The adaptive-volatility model, which scaled its forecast ranges by recent volatility, helped LLY but not GS (Table~\ref{tab:stock_validation}). Volatility, unlike direction, persists enough to be forecast from recent returns~\cite{cont2001,andersen1998}, and LLY's volatility stayed above JumpHMM's level in both test periods. Each JumpHMM forecast started from the model's long-run state probabilities, so the width of its forecasts stayed at the volatility of its 2014--2024 fit. The standard deviation of LLY's daily returns was 1.5 times that level in 2025 and 1.3 times in August and September 2026 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). It estimated volatility as an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) of recent squared returns. This estimate tracked those levels and widened LLY's forecast ranges toward the observed spread of outcomes. The wider ranges improved LLY's continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) in both periods. CRPS rewards forecast distributions concentrated near the observed outcome. Even so, the adaptive-volatility model's 90\% ranges still contained fewer than 90\% of the 2025 outcomes (Table~\ref{tab:stock_validation}). GS's 2025 daily volatility was within 10\% of the JumpHMM level, so adapting it changed the score little (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). In August and September 2026, the standard deviation of GS's daily returns was 1.40\% (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). That was below both the 1.80\% JumpHMM level and the adaptive-volatility estimate, which April--July returns had raised to an average of 2.26\%. The 90\% ranges of both models already contained every 2026 GS outcome (Table~\ref{tab:stock_validation}). The adaptive-volatility model's wider ranges therefore made its forecasts less precise without making them more reliable, and CRPS penalizes that trade~\cite{gneiting2007calibration}. The evidence therefore favors widening JumpHMM's forecast ranges when a ticker's recent volatility exceeds the model's level. Neither the adaptive-volatility model nor the direction-predicting model forecast better on average, so we kept JumpHMM as the example stock model and reported both as comparisons.

Two further problems lie outside what the forecast comparisons can test. The binomial tree prices each option on its own, so nothing forces prices to be consistent across strikes. Some GS prices violated no-arbitrage conditions across strikes, even when IV was set directly from the fitted surface (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:strike_audit}). For a strategy that holds several options, those inconsistencies matter because decisions depend on relative prices as well as the value of each position. Surface methods that impose no-arbitrage constraints offer one way to meet that requirement before simulated prices are used in place of market data~\cite{cont2023}. Fitting the surfaces posed a second problem. The neural surfaces fitted IV more closely than the parametric surface across all observations (Table~\ref{tab:three_way}). Yet on May 11, one of the dates used for fitting, they overpredicted near-the-money IV for MSFT and LLY. The surface fitted on 68 trading days through July 31 showed a related error on later dates. Daily errors showed that LLY's overprediction on later dates began after a scheduled earnings report. The surface fitted through July underpredicted LLY's IV by 17.90 volatility points on August 4, the trading day before the August 5 report. It then overpredicted LLY's IV by 2.04 to 8.07 points on every observed day from August 6 through September 4 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). Option prices have long shown this pattern, with IV rising before an anticipated announcement and falling after it~\cite{patell1979,dubinsky2019}. Each ticker's fitted IV level averaged over the fitting period, so it could not represent IV both before and after a report. GS's July 14 report lay inside the fitting period, and its next report came after the test period. Its daily errors on later dates ranged from 3.07 points below to 3.59 points above observed IV. Averaged over the five trading days before and after each ticker's latest report, the surface's error shifted by 3.36 points for GS, compared with 16.45 points for LLY (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). The May 11 errors fit the same explanation. MSFT and LLY, the two tickers whose IV the surfaces overpredicted, reported on April 29 and April 30, after ten of the fifteen fitting days (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:smile_date_bias}). Adding the time to scheduled earnings reports as a surface input reduced error when surfaces fitted on earlier April dates were tested on April 23 and 24 (Table~\ref{tab:earnings_holdout}). Refitting the July surfaces with the same inputs cut LLY's overprediction after its report from 6.17 to 3.85 points, with both values averaged across three training seeds (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:earnings_refit}). That reduction fell short of the halving we had set in advance as support. Report timing therefore explained part of LLY's error but not all of it. The remainder is consistent with each sector network sharing one earnings response among tickers whose IV moved by different amounts around their reports. In the forecast tests, rescaling the surface to the IV observed on each forecast date removed the starting level error but left the inconsistencies across strikes in place. Pricing each option with the IV that Alpaca, the data vendor, reported on the forecast's end date reduced errors further, although it used information from that later date (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_repricing}). Accurate IV surfaces, accurate stock forecasts, accurate option prices, and prices consistent across strikes are therefore separate requirements, and a synthetic option market needs all four.

The data were the main limitation. All option quotes came from Alpaca's free indicative feed, which is derived from exchange quotes. Our price errors therefore measured agreement with Alpaca's quotes, not with prices at which trades could execute, and the download times did not tie stock and option prices to the same moment. The observation windows were also short. The surface comparisons used fifteen daily snapshots, the later-date tests trained each network with one random seed, and the option forecasts covered 23 trading days. Overlapping forecast periods and shared market moves further reduced the independent information in these tests. The collection kept only some expiration ranges each day, so the one-month options we first selected had no quotes five trading days later (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_availability}). The shorter-dated options that replaced them could not validate the one-month examples. Several conclusions also rest on thin evidence. We proposed the adaptive-volatility and direction-predicting models after seeing the August and September misses. The earnings explanation rested on a few reports for three tickers. We also did not check the cap on JumpHMM's daily returns against the largest real stock moves. The IV update's parameters stayed fixed, and the two extensions that would tie the IV level to JumpHMM's hidden state and its tail visits stayed switched off. The present results therefore support controlled comparisons of IV assumptions and the specific forecasts reported here, not trading use. Complete daily histories of the same options, collected across different market conditions, would allow the forecast tests to be repeated on one-month options and the switched-off extensions to be tested.
