\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Separating the IV update from the stock model allowed controlled comparisons of option values under different IV assumptions. We compared five IV variants for short positions in four out-of-the-money one-month options: a put and a call on each of Goldman Sachs (GS) and Eli Lilly (LLY), with entry premiums set to their April 28, 2026 quote midpoints. In this April 28 comparison, we generated stock paths with the jump hidden Markov model JumpHMM and repriced the options along the same paths under each variant. Frozen IV held each option's IV at its starting value. The direct surface set IV each day to the prediction of a neural IV surface fitted to that ticker, and we call the square of this prediction the target. In the other three variants, each option carried its own simulated implied variance, its IV factor, which moved partway toward the target each day. The mean-reversion variant made only this move. The uncoupled factor added random shocks unrelated to the stock, and the full factor, the complete update we propose, added shocks correlated with stock returns. Because the variants shared the stock paths, the options, and the pricing tree, any difference in option values came from the IV variant alone. All five started from the target, so their values on the entry date agreed with one another, although not with the quoted midpoints. We did not model early assignment, so payoffs at expiration depended only on the final stock price and also agreed. We valued the positions after ten trading days and measured each variant against the direct surface, which followed the target with no lag and no shocks. Differences from it therefore showed the effects of lag and shocks. The surfaces were fitted on quotes through May 11, after the entry date, so this comparison shows how the variants behave rather than forecasting prices.

The IV dynamics changed prices and tail losses before expiration even when average profit and loss (P\&L) changed little. The mean-reversion variant left prices nearly as far from those of the direct surface as frozen IV did (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). The assumed mean-reversion rate closed only about 6\% of the gap to the target each trading day, while the target moved with the stock price and the time to expiration. Random shocks then moved prices on individual paths much more than they changed average P\&L, because their effects largely canceled across paths. Correlating the shocks with stock returns changed the tail of the P\&L. The full factor linked shocks to stock returns with a parameter set to $-0.6$ by assumption. As a result, IV tended to rise on days the stock fell, consistent with the leverage effect~\cite{cont2001}. On falling-stock paths, higher IV raised put values and deepened the losses of short puts. On rising-stock paths, lower IV reduced call values and softened the losses of short calls (Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamic_ablation}). Compared with the uncoupled factor at the fixed ten-day horizon, the average loss in the worst 5\% of outcomes increased for both short puts and decreased for both short calls. Each change was at least seven times its simulation standard error (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). These comparisons showed how IV assumptions affected simulated losses at a fixed horizon, even when mean P\&L changed little and expiration payoffs agreed. They established sensitivity to the chosen dynamics; they did not test an exit rule or establish that those dynamics matched the market.

More flexible IV surfaces improved the fit to calibration quotes, and adding earnings information reduced some errors on withheld dates. The per-ticker surfaces were the closest fits to the calibration quotes (Table~\ref{tab:three_way}), yet they overpredicted near-the-money IV for Microsoft (MSFT) and LLY on May 11, the last fitting date (Fig.~\ref{fig:per_ticker_panels}). Option prices have long shown IV rising before an anticipated announcement and falling after it~\cite{patell1979,dubinsky2019}. None of the surfaces used by the simulator had an earnings input, and each held a ticker's IV level constant across its fitting dates. Ten of the fifteen calibration snapshots preceded both companies' April reports, so the fitted level could remain above the IV observed afterward. The sector surfaces fitted through July 31 likewise underpredicted LLY's IV before its August 5 report and overpredicted it on every observed day of the following month (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). Adding earnings inputs reduced errors in the April holdout and reduced LLY's average overprediction after the August report by 38\% in the exploratory refit, falling short of the protocol's halving criterion (Table~\ref{tab:earnings_holdout}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:earnings_refit}). Adding earnings inputs reduced LLY's bias, although substantial error remained. Accurate individual prices would also be insufficient for strategies holding several options, whose decisions depend on relative prices. Under the direct surface, simulated GS prices failed 178 of 1{,}818 convexity checks by more than \$0.01 per share, implying negative-priced butterfly spreads (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:strike_audit}). We did not constrain the surfaces to be free of arbitrage, and we priced each option separately. This separation made the effects of IV assumptions easy to isolate but left consistency across strikes unenforced. The static no-arbitrage constraints used in methods that simulate IV surfaces offer a starting point~\cite{cont2023}. Those constraints are stated for European prices, whereas the simulator prices American options, so its prices would still need checks like ours.

The forecast diagnostics identified stock prediction as a major source of option-price error, while the stock benchmarks showed that adapting volatility improved LLY's forecast distributions. In the five-day forecasts, the full factor was only slightly more accurate than frozen IV when scored by the mean absolute error of the average over paths, and its small advantage was not consistent across dates (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_main}). Scored by the median over paths, it was no more accurate than frozen IV (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:option_point_summary}). All variants started from observed IV, with each target rescaled to match that starting value, keeping initial surface-level errors out of the comparison. The slow update closed only about a quarter of a gap to a fixed target within five trading days, and random shocks partly canceled across paths, so average IV stayed near the value that frozen IV held fixed (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_checks}). With a fixed surface, rate, shock size, and correlation, the update could not anticipate shifts in the observed surface's level, skew, and curvature~\cite{cont2002}. Stock prediction was another major source of error: repeating forecasts along the stock paths that actually occurred cut the full factor's option errors 3.8-fold for GS and 5.4-fold for LLY on the thirteen dates per ticker with complete histories (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:forecast_conditional}). This was a diagnostic using future information, and frozen IV still had lower errors on those paths. Neither JumpHMM nor the adaptive-volatility stock model materially improved point forecasts over assuming no price change, while historical tuning set the direction-predicting model's drift to zero (Table~\ref{tab:stock_validation}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:stock_selection}). The adaptive-volatility model improved LLY's forecast distributions in both 2025 and 2026, but worsened GS's. The LLY improvement was consistent with evidence that volatility is partly predictable from recent returns~\cite{cont2001,andersen1998}. Its wider ranges tracked LLY's higher volatility, whereas JumpHMM and the adaptive-volatility model already covered every GS outcome in 2026, so wider ranges added no coverage~\cite{gneiting2007calibration}. Improving forecast uncertainty was therefore distinct from predicting direction, and neither the IV update nor the stock alternatives established a general forecasting advantage.

The simulator provided a reproducible way to compare IV and stock assumptions, with conclusions bounded by the available data and modeling choices. All option quotes came from Alpaca's free indicative feed, which is derived from exchange quotes. Indicative quotes are not firm offers, so our price errors measured agreement with Alpaca's quotes, not with prices at which trades could execute. Stock and option prices were also not recorded at exactly the same moment, which may affect every IV and price error. The observation windows were short, and five-day option forecasts were scored on only 17 sessions. Overlapping forecast periods and shared market moves further reduced the independent information in these tests. Missing exact-contract outcomes also limited five-day forecasts to shorter-maturity options; complete daily histories would allow the test to be repeated on one-month options. Apart from the earnings refit, each network tested on later dates was trained with a single random seed. We proposed the alternative stock models after seeing JumpHMM's August and September forecast misses, so their 2026 comparison was not a blind test. The earnings explanation rested on a few reports and two tests that left substantial error. Widening the cutoff on JumpHMM's daily returns changed the reported scores little, but did not establish robustness to the largest real stock moves. The IV update's rate, shock size, and correlation were assumed rather than estimated, and its target level did not depend on the stock model's hidden state. Quotes spanning calm and turbulent markets would allow those state-dependent levels to be estimated and tested. Together with prices consistent across strikes and better forecasts of changes in IV and stock uncertainty, these data would support a stronger assessment of the simulator. The present evidence supports comparisons of modeling assumptions, not trading decisions, and its forecast conclusions apply only to the options and dates tested here.
