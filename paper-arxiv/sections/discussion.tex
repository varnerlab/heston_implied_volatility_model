\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The study supports a modular synthetic-option workflow. Offline market IV calibration supplied a positive surface, JumpHMM supplied non-Gaussian stock paths, contract-specific square-root factors supplied dynamic lattice IV, and American lattices supplied prices and Greeks. This organization removed the need for a contemporaneous option quote at each simulated step. It did not remove the need for historical option data, and it did not produce IV from equity returns alone. The fitted IV surface remained an empirical input learned from option ladders.

The calibration comparisons showed two useful patterns. First, replacing the five-parameter global surface with a neural surface reduced pooled in-sample error. Second, sector sharing improved further on the global network, while per-ticker fits helped many data-rich names. These comparisons concern the fitted product $\theta_i\psi_i$. The level and neural shape are not separately identified because the network output contains an additive bias. A normalization constraint would be required before comparing estimated levels across tickers or assigning economic meaning to either component.

The temporal checks were informative but narrow. On the May 11 holdout, pooled test RMSE exceeded train RMSE by 0.68 volatility percentage points. On the April 23 and April 24 holdout, earnings inputs reduced test RMSE by 1.05 points. Removing event-adjacent rows produced a much easier subset, but it also changed most of the training and test observations. The exclusion therefore cannot identify earnings as the cause of the full generalization gap. Repeated date blocks, matched samples, and multiple training seeds would provide a stronger test.

The scenario results separate interim valuation from terminal payoff risk. The dynamic IV factors changed option marks and Greeks before expiration. Once the contracts reached expiration, Equation~\eqref{eq:terminal_pnl} depended only on the JumpHMM terminal stock price, strike, and collected premium. The call-versus-put tail differences in Figs.~\ref{fig:gs_pnl} and~\ref{fig:lly_pnl} therefore describe the fitted JumpHMM marginals relative to the selected contracts. They are not evidence that the neural IV skew or factor coupling caused terminal P\&L asymmetry.

Several extensions could address the present scope. Static no-arbitrage penalties could impose strike convexity and calendar consistency during surface training. A normalization such as zero mean log shape on a reference grid could identify the ticker level relative to shape. State-conditioned option histories could test the proposed JumpHMM-state and aggregate-mood terms that remained inactive here. Finally, repeated rolling-origin experiments could evaluate day-specific adaptation, including earnings events and unscheduled volatility shifts, without training on the scenario date.

The study has important limitations. The surface networks enforced positivity but not static arbitrage. The GS and LLY examples used only 1{,}000 paths, one random seed, two tickers, one maturity window, and fixed $\kappa$, $\sigma_v$, and $\rho$ values that were not calibrated. Both surfaces used the full corpus, so neither example was out of sample. The pricing assumptions set dividend yield to zero and omitted discrete dividends, transaction costs, bid-ask execution, margin, and early assignment. The held-to-expiration P\&L analysis did not test hedging or mark-to-market liquidation. The state and mood mechanisms were inactive, and the model was not a standard Heston joint stock-variance process. These restrictions limit the results to a software and modeling illustration rather than a validated trading or risk system.
