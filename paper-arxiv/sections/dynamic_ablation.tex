We therefore compared five IV variants on identical stock paths for the four scenario contracts and found that IV dynamics changed interim option values and the lower tail of liquidation P\&L (Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamic_ablation}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). The comparison held contracts, entry premiums, and pricing assumptions fixed so that differences in interim marks could be attributed to the IV module. After ten trading transitions, the full factor changed marks by a mean absolute \$1.77--\$2.41 per share relative to direct surface evaluation. For the GS put, the \$1.77 mean absolute change was about 35 times the \$0.05 decrease in mean P\&L, which had a paired Monte Carlo standard error of \$0.04, so positive and negative differences largely canceled when averaged across paths. Frozen IV and deterministic mean reversion also produced different marks from the direct surface, showing that movement of the target and persistence around it affected valuation even without stochastic shocks. Comparing the coupled and uncoupled factors on the same paths showed how return coupling changed valuation across stock outcomes, with short-put P\&L declining most on falling-stock paths and short-call P\&L improving most on rising-stock paths for both tickers (Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamic_ablation}). Coupling also changed the lower tails of short-position P\&L, which we measured with 5\% expected shortfall, defined as mean P\&L at or below its 5\% quantile, so that more negative values indicate larger simulated losses. Negative coupling moved the GS put's expected shortfall from $-\$62.98$ under uncoupled factors to $-\$64.60$ under the full factor, whereas the call's improved from $-\$71.53$ to $-\$69.82$, and the LLY contracts showed the same direction of change (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). The full-factor mean absolute mark differences were similar across the three evaluation seeds (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ablation_seeds}), and all variants produced identical entry marks and terminal payoffs. The experiment thus located the contribution of IV dynamics in interim valuation and liquidation risk while preserving the stock paths and terminal outcomes.

We then refined the lattice and audited strike consistency, and found that the ablation differences exceeded numerical error but that the GS prices violated some necessary strike conditions (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:strike_audit}). Increasing lattice depth from 201 to 401 changed sampled marks by at most \$0.0017 per share, more than two orders of magnitude below the mean absolute differences among IV variants. No factor reached the variance floor in the four scenario contracts, although current strike/spot left the training interval on 0.7--2.9\% of pre-expiry path dates across ticker, contract, and seed combinations, so some marks used extrapolated surface values. The strike audit tested necessary conditions across separately priced contracts. At 401 lattice steps, the direct GS surface exceeded the \$0.01 convexity tolerance in 178 of 1{,}818 tested strike triples, with a largest excess of \$0.113 per share, and the GS full factor had three monotonicity violations among 2{,}020 adjacent-strike comparisons, with a maximum of \$0.013. These violations persisted at the higher lattice depth, whereas no LLY variant violated the tested conditions at the stated tolerance. Single-contract lattice accuracy therefore did not ensure consistency across strikes, and the residual GS violations limited the use of the generated prices as market-consistent training data even though the same outputs supported controlled comparisons of IV assumptions.
