The fitted scenarios establish that the modules produce contract paths, but terminal payoffs cannot reveal the contribution of dynamic IV. We therefore held stock paths fixed and compared five IV variants at interim liquidation dates (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_methods}). After ten trading transitions, the full factor changed marks relative to the direct surface by a mean absolute \$1.77 and \$1.96 per share for the GS put and call, and \$1.95 and \$2.41 for the LLY put and call (Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamic_ablation}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). The corresponding GS put mean P\&L difference was only $-\$0.05$, with paired Monte Carlo standard error \$0.04. Thus substantial pathwise changes could cancel in the mean. The frozen and deterministic mean-reversion variants also differed from direct surface evaluation, showing that persistence around a moving target contributed separately from stochastic shocks. The full-factor mean absolute differences were similar across the three seeds (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ablation_seeds}). Entry marks and terminal payoffs agreed exactly across every variant.

The coupling comparison located a distinct change in downside exposure. Relative to uncoupled stochastic factors, negative return coupling moved the GS put's ten-transition expected shortfall from $-\$63.24$ to $-\$64.86$ and the call's from $-\$71.54$ to $-\$69.83$ (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). LLY showed the same direction: put expected shortfall moved from $-\$35.88$ to $-\$38.44$, while call expected shortfall moved from $-\$48.47$ to $-\$45.17$. These are descriptive changes within the imposed model, not evidence that one variant forecast market risk more accurately. The largest sampled mark change from increasing the lattice depth from 201 to 401 was \$0.0017 per share, much smaller than the ablation differences. No factor reached the variance floor in the four scenario contracts; current strike/spot left the training interval on 0.7--2.9\% of pre-expiry path dates across ticker, contract, and seed combinations.

The strike audit identified a separate limit on consistency. At 401 steps, the direct GS surface violated the \$0.01 convexity tolerance in 178 of 1{,}818 tested strike triples, with a maximum excess of \$0.113 per share; the full factor had three monotonicity violations among 2{,}020 adjacent-strike comparisons, with a maximum of \$0.013 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:strike_audit}). Violations persisted at the higher lattice depth. No LLY variant violated the tested conditions at the stated tolerance. These sampled diagnostics show that the full pipeline can produce strike inconsistencies even when each contract is priced by an American lattice. They support using the outputs as controlled scenarios with explicit diagnostics, while arbitrage-constrained surfaces and broader temporal validation remain necessary for market-consistent training data.
