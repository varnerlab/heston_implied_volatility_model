We therefore repriced the four scenario options under five IV variants on identical stock paths and found that the IV update changed option values before expiration and the lower tail of early-closing P\&L (Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamic_ablation}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). Contracts, entry premiums, and pricing assumptions stayed fixed, so any price difference came from the IV update. After ten trading days, the full factor changed prices by a mean absolute \$1.77--\$2.41 per share relative to the direct surface. For the GS put, the \$1.77 mean absolute change was about 38 times the \$0.05 decrease in mean P\&L, which had a paired Monte Carlo standard error of \$0.04. Positive and negative differences therefore largely canceled across paths. Frozen IV and deterministic mean reversion also gave prices that differed from the direct surface. The moving target, and the lag with which the factor followed it, therefore affected prices even without random shocks. Comparing the coupled and uncoupled factors on the same paths isolated the effect of linking IV shocks to stock returns (Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamic_ablation}). For both tickers, short-put P\&L declined most on falling-stock paths, and short-call P\&L improved most on rising-stock paths. Coupling also changed the lower tail of short-position P\&L, measured by 5\% expected shortfall, where more negative values mean larger losses. Negative coupling moved the GS put's expected shortfall from $-\$62.98$ under the uncoupled factor to $-\$64.60$ under the full factor. The GS call's expected shortfall improved from $-\$71.53$ to $-\$69.82$, and the LLY options moved in the same directions (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). The full factor's mean absolute price differences were similar across the three evaluation seeds (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ablation_seeds}). All variants gave identical entry prices and payoffs at expiration.

We then refined the tree and checked prices across strikes, and found that the variant differences exceeded numerical error but that some GS prices violated necessary strike conditions (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:ablation_seeds} and~\ref{tab:strike_audit}). Increasing the tree from 201 to 401 steps changed sampled prices by at most \$0.0017 per share (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ablation_seeds}). That change was more than two orders of magnitude below the mean absolute differences among IV variants (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:dynamic_ablation}). No factor reached the variance floor in the four scenario options (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ablation_seeds}). Strike/spot did leave the training interval on 0.7--2.9\% of path dates before expiration across tickers, options, and seeds, so some prices used extrapolated surface values (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ablation_seeds}). At 401 steps, the GS direct surface exceeded the \$0.01 convexity tolerance in 178 of 1{,}818 tested strike triples, with a largest excess of \$0.113 per share (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:strike_audit_depth}). Each convexity violation corresponds to a butterfly spread priced below zero. The GS full factor had three monotonicity violations among 2{,}020 adjacent-strike comparisons, with a maximum of \$0.013 (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:strike_audit_depth}). These violations persisted at the higher tree depth (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:strike_audit_depth}), whereas no LLY variant violated the tested conditions at the stated tolerance. Pricing each option accurately on its own therefore left the GS prices inconsistent across strikes.
