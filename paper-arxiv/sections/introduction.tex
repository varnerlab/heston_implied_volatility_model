\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Synthetic option markets support portfolio stress tests, trading-system studies, and machine-learning experiments that require complete paths of option values and risk sensitivities under unobserved market conditions. For American options, these paths must also account for changing time to expiration and early exercise. A plausible stock path is therefore insufficient: the simulation must assign volatility across strikes and maturities, update it as the path unfolds, and convert each state into an American-option value and Greeks.
In observed data, implied volatility (IV) is recovered by inverting an option price under a chosen pricing model~\cite{black1973}. After calibration, many models can generate future prices without future market quotes. Our objective was to retain that capability while allowing the physical stock-return generator to be chosen independently of the option-pricing model. This separation is useful when an empirical, non-Gaussian return model is selected for its tail behavior or volatility clustering, but a simulation also needs option marks across strikes and maturities.

Existing approaches address this problem through different modeling choices. Parametric surfaces such as SABR and SVI summarize observed volatility smiles~\cite{hagan2002,gatheral2004}, while neural methods can accelerate pricing and calibration within specified volatility models~\cite{horvath2021,bayer2019}. Direct models of IV-surface dynamics describe changes in surface level, skew, and curvature through common factors~\cite{cont2002}. More recent simulation methods combine statistical surface dynamics with static arbitrage constraints~\cite{cont2023}. These approaches establish that IV surfaces can be simulated directly; generation without future quotes is therefore not itself the novelty of the present work. Local- and stochastic-volatility models provide joint stock and volatility dynamics~\cite{dupire1994,derman1994,heston1993}, whereas our construction preserves a separately selected physical return generator and connects it to contract-level IV factors and an American lattice.
Return-first methods have the complementary limitation. Historical and generative return models can reproduce non-Gaussian features of equity data, including heavy tails and volatility clustering~\cite{cont2001,wiese2020}. A Jump Hidden Markov Model (JumpHMM), for example, represents returns through persistent latent states and explicit visits to tail states~\cite{jumphmm2025}. Such a generator supplies physical stock paths but does not determine an option-implied volatility surface or an American-option value. Recombining lattices solve the early-exercise problem once spot, maturity, rates, and volatility have been supplied~\cite{cox1979,leisen1996}, but the lattice does not generate the volatility input. Thus the return generator, IV model, and option pricer can each be well specified while the synthetic market remains incomplete.

The contribution is a modular implementation that connects an empirical return generator, fitted contract-level IV dynamics, and American-option valuation through explicit interfaces. Market option data enter during the offline surface fit. The return generator then supplies spot paths, the IV module supplies contract volatility states, and the lattice calculates marks and Greeks. We compared global, sector-specific, and per-ticker surface sharing on 234{,}549 observations from 31 tickers and fifteen dates, and tested temporal splits with and without earnings-calendar inputs. Fitted Goldman Sachs and Eli Lilly scenarios demonstrated the resulting contract paths. A paired dynamic ablation then held stock paths fixed while changing the IV module, measuring its effect on interim marks and early-liquidation profit and loss. These experiments evaluated fitted scenarios and model sensitivity; prospective accuracy and joint no-arbitrage consistency require separate validation.
