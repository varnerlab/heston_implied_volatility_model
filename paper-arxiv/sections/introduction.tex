\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Implied volatility (IV) is the volatility that makes a pricing model, such as that of Black and Scholes~\cite{black1973}, reproduce an observed option price~\cite{latane1976}. An option's IV is therefore backed out of its market price rather than observed on its own. A simulated stock path has no option prices, so it has no IV either. Without an IV, a pricing model cannot value an option on a simulated date. A stock portfolio can be stress-tested or backtested on simulated price paths directly, because each simulated price is the position's value. An option position cannot, because its value on each simulated date also depends on an IV that the simulation does not supply. Those dates matter. Option strategies are judged by their payoffs at expiration but managed on every date before it. A short put that expires worthless can still lose money if the stock falls first and the trader has to close or hedge the position. American options make the earlier dates matter even more, since the holder may exercise on any of them. A simulation that values options along stock paths therefore needs two parts. The first is a stock model that reproduces the return behavior the stress test or backtest depends on, such as heavy tails and volatility clustering~\cite{cont2001}. The second is a rule that assigns every option an IV across strikes and expiration dates and updates it as the simulated stock moves.

Existing approaches address different parts of this problem through their choices of return dynamics, volatility representation, and pricing model. Parametric models such as stochastic alpha beta rho (SABR) and stochastic volatility inspired (SVI) provide compact descriptions of volatility smiles~\cite{hagan2002,gatheral2004}. Neural networks can learn the pricing map of a specified volatility model, which speeds up its pricing and calibration~\cite{horvath2021,bayer2019}. Models of IV-surface dynamics describe changes in level, skew, and curvature through common factors~\cite{cont2002}, with more recent methods incorporating static arbitrage constraints into surface simulation~\cite{cont2023}. Local- and stochastic-volatility models instead place stock and volatility dynamics within a joint pricing model~\cite{dupire1994,derman1994,heston1993}. Each of these can price options at strikes, expiration dates, or market states that were never quoted. Most, however, either describe the smile on a single date or bring stock dynamics of their own. They are not built to take stock paths from a separately chosen generator as given. That limitation matters when a researcher has already chosen a stock-return generator for how well it matches real returns and wants to vary only the volatility assumptions. Synthetic return generators, from regime-switching models with jumps to generative neural networks, can reproduce important features of equity returns~\cite{cont2001,wiese2020,jumphmm2025}. Their paths carry no option prices. What is missing is a rule that takes each simulated stock path as given and assigns every option an IV along it. Such a rule would let researchers compare volatility assumptions on identical stock paths. It would be useful only if the resulting prices matched market quotes and stayed free of arbitrage across strikes and expiration dates.

In this study, we built a simulator that takes stock paths from any synthetic generator and assigns every American option an IV along them. Each day, the simulator moves every option's IV partway toward a target read from an IV surface fitted to market quotes and adds random shocks partly tied to the stock's moves. A binomial tree then converts that IV into the option's price~\cite{cox1979,leisen1996}. We used the simulator to ask three questions. The first was whether fitted surfaces were accurate enough to serve as these targets. They served that role only in part. Surfaces fitted separately to sectors or single stocks matched the quotes better than one surface for all stocks, but their accuracy on later dates was uneven. The second was whether the way IV was updated mattered when the stock paths were held fixed. It did. Updates ranging from fixed IV to the full update with random shocks gave different option values before expiration and different worst losses from closing short positions early. An analysis of payoffs at expiration alone would miss these differences. The third was whether the simulator could forecast later option prices. With the full IV update, it forecast them only slightly better than with IV held fixed, and most of the error came from the stock forecast, which no IV update can correct. Some simulated prices were also inconsistent across strikes, and consistency across expiration dates was not tested. The simulator is therefore better suited to controlled comparisons of volatility assumptions than to forecasting or to replacing market data. More broadly, the approach turns any synthetic stock generator into a source of option scenarios for stress tests and backtests on hypothetical market paths.
