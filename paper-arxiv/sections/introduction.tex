\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Evaluating an option strategy requires a model of how contract values change as market conditions evolve. A position that appears profitable at expiration may incur losses before that date, when a trader may need to close or hedge it, so stress tests and trading-system studies require paths of option values and risk sensitivities as well as paths of the underlying stock price. American options add a further requirement because their values also reflect the opportunity to exercise before expiration. Historical option quotes provide observations of these quantities over realized market conditions, but a simulation must supply them for stock paths that have not occurred. Implied volatility (IV) provides a useful connection between market quotes and an option-pricing model because it is the volatility input recovered by inverting an observed price~\cite{black1973}. To use this connection in a synthetic market, the simulator needs a rule for assigning IV across strikes and maturities and updating those values as the stock path unfolds. The stock model must also represent the return behavior relevant to the intended experiment, including features such as heavy tails and volatility clustering~\cite{cont2001}. The resulting problem is to simulate each option's value on every date along each stock path in a way that shows how that value responds to the stock scenario.

Existing approaches address different parts of this problem through their choices of return dynamics, volatility representation, and pricing model. Parametric models such as stochastic alpha beta rho (SABR) and stochastic volatility inspired (SVI) provide compact descriptions of volatility smiles~\cite{hagan2002,gatheral2004}. Neural methods allow more flexible representations and can accelerate pricing and calibration within specified volatility models~\cite{horvath2021,bayer2019}. Models of IV-surface dynamics describe changes in level, skew, and curvature through common factors~\cite{cont2002}, with more recent methods incorporating static arbitrage constraints into surface simulation~\cite{cont2023}. Local- and stochastic-volatility models instead place stock and volatility dynamics within a joint pricing model~\cite{dupire1994,derman1994,heston1993}. These approaches can already generate option prices beyond the observed quotes. A different design question arises when the researcher wants to retain a physical stock-return model chosen for its empirical behavior and vary the volatility assumptions separately. Historical and generative return models can reproduce important features of equity data~\cite{cont2001,wiese2020}, but their stock paths alone do not specify an option-implied volatility surface. For example, the Jump Hidden Markov Model (JumpHMM) generates returns through Markov transitions among return states and explicit visits to tail states~\cite{jumphmm2025}. Those returns determine what an option pays at expiration but leave its IV, and therefore its value, on earlier dates unspecified. A model that updates each option's IV along the stock path can provide that input to a binomial tree that prices American options~\cite{cox1979,leisen1996}. This separation allows researchers to compare volatility assumptions on the same stock paths, provided that the accuracy of the resulting option prices and their consistency across strikes and expiration dates are checked explicitly.

In this study, we built a simulator that values American options along stock paths generated by JumpHMM. Before simulation, we fitted IV surfaces, which predict IV from an option's moneyness (strike divided by stock price) and time to expiration, to observed option quotes. On each simulated date, every option's implied variance then moved partway toward the surface prediction for its current moneyness and remaining time, following the mean-reverting variance equation of the Heston model~\cite{heston1993} with random shocks that tended to raise IV when the stock fell, and a binomial tree converted that IV into the option's value and price sensitivities. The stock model stayed unchanged, and the simulation used no option quotes from after the fitting period. Comparing one surface for all tickers, one per sector, and one per ticker on 234{,}549 observations from 31 tickers, we found that more flexible surfaces fitted to fewer tickers each had lower in-sample IV error, although on individual dates the surfaces still missed the overall IV level of some tickers and those misses carried into dollar prices. Accuracy on later dates was uneven, since errors varied widely across 53 tests that refitted the surfaces on all earlier dates and scored the next date, and they rose for surfaces fitted through July 2026 and tested on August and September quotes. Adding the time to scheduled earnings reports as a surface input reduced error when surfaces fitted on earlier April dates were tested on April 23 and 24. We then simulated Goldman Sachs (GS) and Eli Lilly (LLY) options and repriced the same stock paths under five ways of updating IV, from holding IV fixed at its starting value to the full update with random shocks, and found that the choice changed option values before expiration and the lower tail of profit and loss (P\&L) from closing short positions early, while starting values and payoffs at expiration agreed exactly. Doubling the number of tree steps changed prices by less than 1\% of these differences, so they were not numerical error, whereas a check of prices across strikes found GS prices that violated basic no-arbitrage conditions, mostly butterfly spreads priced below zero, and these violations remained with the finer tree. Forecasts of later GS and LLY option prices showed that the full IV update predicted option values almost no better than holding IV fixed, and repeating the forecasts with the stock paths that actually occurred, information unavailable when a forecast was made, cut option errors several-fold and showed that errors in the stock forecast caused most of the misses. We therefore compared JumpHMM with two simple stock models whose settings we chose on earlier data, one with volatility that adapted to recent returns and one that also predicted direction from recent returns, and found that every model predicted stock prices about as accurately as assuming no change, both in 2025 and in August and September 2026, whereas the adaptive-volatility model improved the predicted range of stock prices for LLY but not GS. Taken together, these results showed that the framework supported controlled comparisons of IV assumptions on the same stock paths, that neither a more elaborate IV update nor the tested stock models forecast better than simpler alternatives, and that prices consistent across strikes and expiration dates remained a separate requirement before simulated prices could stand in for market prices.
