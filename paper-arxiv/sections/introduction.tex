\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Stress tests and backtests of trading strategies can run on simulated stock paths as well as on historical prices. For a stock portfolio, the simulated prices directly determine the value of its holdings. Valuing options before expiration requires an additional pricing model. A common approach uses the option's implied volatility (IV), the volatility at which a pricing model, such as the Black--Scholes model~\citep{black1973}, reproduces the option's market price~\citep{latane1976}. A simulated stock path comes with no option prices from which to infer IV. The simulated stock's own volatility does not specify the IV of every option, because market IVs differ across strikes and expiration dates. For a given put or call exercised at expiration, the payoff depends only on the terminal stock price. Option positions, however, can be adjusted or closed before expiration. To value them with an IV-based pricing model, a simulator needs two parts. The first is a stock-return model that generates paths with features of observed returns, such as heavy tails and volatility clustering~\citep{cont2001}. Regime-switching models with jumps and generative neural networks have been used to model these features~\citep{wiese2020,jumphmm2025}. The second is a rule that assigns every option an IV and updates it as the simulated stock moves and expiration approaches.

Several families of models, once fitted to quotes, can assign an IV or a price to options that were not quoted. Parametric smile formulas, such as those derived from the stochastic alpha beta rho (SABR) model and the stochastic volatility inspired (SVI) form, describe the smile on a given date with a few parameters~\citep{hagan2002,gatheral2004}. Models of IV-surface dynamics describe how the surface's level, skew, and curvature change over time through common random drivers that move IVs across the surface~\citep{cont2002}. More recent surface-dynamics methods prevent static arbitrage, or inconsistencies among option prices on the same date, in simulated surfaces~\citep{cont2023}. When used as full pricing models, local- and stochastic-volatility models generate the stock and its volatility together within one pricing model~\citep{dupire1994,derman1994,heston1993}. Neural networks trained to imitate such models speed up pricing and calibration while retaining the pricing assumptions of the model they approximate~\citep{horvath2021,bayer2019}. We focused on how the choice of IV rule changes option values when the stock-return model and its simulated paths stay fixed. To make this comparison, we needed an update whose response to stock returns and additional random changes could be removed separately. Simple choices include holding each option's IV fixed or evaluating a previously fitted surface at its current moneyness and remaining time to expiration. With the surface parameters held fixed, neither rule introduces additional randomness once the stock path is given. Observed IV surfaces, however, also shift in ways the stock price does not explain~\citep{cont2002}. We therefore sought an update that takes the stock path as given, lets IV respond to its returns, and adds random IV changes beyond those responses. Running this update and simpler rules on the same stock paths would isolate how the choice of IV rule changes option values.

In this study, we built a simulator of American options using stock paths from a jump hidden Markov model. We fitted parametric and neural IV surfaces to vendor quotes before simulation, then held their parameters fixed. Each option's implied variance, the square of IV, followed a daily update adapted from the Heston variance equation~\citep{heston1993}. The update combined a pull toward the fitted surface's variance prediction with random shocks partly tied to stock returns, so declines tended to raise IV. A binomial tree converted IV into option prices~\citep{cox1979,leisen1996}. We asked four questions. First, we measured how well the surfaces matched reported IV. Neural surfaces fitted by sector or stock had lower overall fitting errors than the global parametric and neural surfaces. They still missed shifts in IV across dates, and sector surfaces had larger errors on withheld dates. Earnings-date inputs reduced some of those errors. Second, we compared IV rules on identical stock paths for Goldman Sachs and Eli Lilly options. The rules changed option values before expiration and the worst simulated short-position losses after ten trading days. With early assignment ignored, expiration payoffs were identical for the same option and stock path. Third, we checked prices for arbitrage across strikes. Some violated the tested conditions, mostly when IV followed the surface prediction directly; the surfaces had not been constrained to prevent these violations. We did not test arbitrage across expiration dates. Fourth, we compared mean simulated option prices with later quote midpoints. Our update reduced average absolute errors only slightly relative to fixed IV. Substituting observed stock paths substantially reduced option-price errors, indicating that stock prediction was a major source of error. The stock generator and alternatives that adapted to recent volatility or attempted to predict return direction all had point-forecast errors close to an unchanged-price benchmark. These results support controlled comparisons of IV rules on fixed stock paths, but neither improved forecasting nor a guarantee of arbitrage-free prices.
