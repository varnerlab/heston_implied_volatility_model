\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Option strategies are judged by their payoffs at expiration but managed on every date before it. A short put that expires worthless can still lose money if the stock falls first and the trader has to close or hedge the position. Stress tests and backtests of trading systems therefore need the value and price sensitivities of each option along the whole stock path, not only the final payoff. American options make the earlier dates matter even more, since the holder may exercise on any of them. Historical quotes record these values, but only along the one path the market actually took. A simulation has to produce them for stock paths that never happened. Implied volatility (IV) is the natural quantity to simulate. It is the volatility that makes a pricing model, such as that of Black and Scholes~\cite{black1973}, reproduce an observed option price~\cite{latane1976}. Once an option's IV is known on a given date, the model therefore returns its value. A synthetic option market therefore needs two parts. The first is a stock model that reproduces the return behavior the experiment depends on, such as heavy tails and volatility clustering~\cite{cont2001}. The second is a rule that assigns every option an IV across strikes and expiration dates and updates it as the simulated stock moves.

Existing approaches address different parts of this problem through their choices of return dynamics, volatility representation, and pricing model. Parametric models such as stochastic alpha beta rho (SABR) and stochastic volatility inspired (SVI) provide compact descriptions of volatility smiles~\cite{hagan2002,gatheral2004}. Neural networks can learn the pricing map of a specified volatility model, which speeds up its pricing and calibration~\cite{horvath2021,bayer2019}. Models of IV-surface dynamics describe changes in level, skew, and curvature through common factors~\cite{cont2002}, with more recent methods incorporating static arbitrage constraints into surface simulation~\cite{cont2023}. Local- and stochastic-volatility models instead place stock and volatility dynamics within a joint pricing model~\cite{dupire1994,derman1994,heston1993}. All of these models can already generate option prices beyond the observed quotes. A different need arises when a researcher wants to keep a stock-return model chosen for how well it matches real returns and vary the volatility assumptions separately. Generative return models can reproduce important features of equity data~\cite{cont2001,wiese2020}, but their stock paths alone do not specify an option-implied volatility surface. The Jump Hidden Markov Model (JumpHMM), the stock model we used in this study, generates returns through Markov transitions among return states and explicit visits to tail states~\cite{jumphmm2025}. A model that updates each option's IV along the stock path can supply the missing input to a binomial tree that prices American options~\cite{cox1979,leisen1996}. This separation allows researchers to compare volatility assumptions on the same stock paths. The resulting option prices must still be checked for accuracy and for no-arbitrage consistency across strikes and expiration dates.

In this study, we built a simulator that values American options along stock paths generated by JumpHMM~\cite{jumphmm2025}. We first fitted IV surfaces to observed option quotes. Each surface predicted an option's IV from its moneyness (strike divided by stock price) and its time to expiration. On each simulated date, every option's implied variance moved partway toward the surface's prediction for its current moneyness and remaining time. This update borrowed the mean-reverting form of the Heston model's variance equation~\cite{heston1993}. It also included random shocks that tended to raise IV when the stock fell. A binomial tree then converted each option's IV into its value and price sensitivities. The simulation used no option quotes from after the fitting period. We compared three levels of surface sharing on 234{,}549 observations from 31 tickers: one surface for all tickers, one per sector, and one per ticker. More flexible surfaces shared by fewer tickers had lower in-sample IV error. Even so, on individual dates the surfaces missed the overall IV level of some tickers, and these misses carried into dollar prices. Accuracy on later dates was uneven across 53 tests that refitted the surfaces on all earlier dates and scored the next date. Error also rose when surfaces fitted through July 2026 were tested on August and September quotes, because a fixed surface could not follow later shifts in a ticker's IV level. Adding the time to scheduled earnings reports as a surface input reduced error on two April dates held out from fitting. We then simulated Goldman Sachs (GS) and Eli Lilly (LLY) options. We repriced the same stock paths under five ways of updating IV, from holding IV fixed to the full update with random shocks. The choice of update changed option values before expiration and the lower tail of profit and loss (P\&L) when short positions were closed early. Starting values and payoffs at expiration were identical under all five. These differences were not numerical error, since doubling the number of tree steps changed prices by a small fraction of them. Some GS prices, however, violated basic no-arbitrage conditions across strikes, mostly butterfly spreads priced below zero, and the finer tree did not remove them. We next forecast later GS and LLY option prices. The full IV update predicted option values almost no better than holding IV fixed. Repeating the forecasts with the stock paths that actually occurred, which no forecaster could have known, cut option errors several-fold. Errors in the stock forecast caused most of the misses. We therefore compared JumpHMM with two stock models tuned on earlier data. The adaptive-volatility model let its volatility track recent returns. The direction-predicting model also forecast the stock's drift from recent stock and market returns. Tuning set that drift to zero, so the two models gave identical forecasts. Every model predicted stock prices about as accurately as assuming no change, both in 2025 and in August and September 2026. The adaptive-volatility model predicted the range of LLY prices better than JumpHMM did, but not that of GS. Overall, the simulator supported controlled comparisons of IV assumptions on the same stock paths but did not forecast better than simpler alternatives. Once its prices are consistent across strikes and expiration dates, such a simulator could let traders and risk managers stress-test option strategies on market paths that never happened.
