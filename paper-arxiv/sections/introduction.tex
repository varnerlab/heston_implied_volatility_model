\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Synthetic option markets support portfolio stress tests, trading-system studies, and machine-learning experiments that require complete paths of option values and risk sensitivities under unobserved market conditions. For American options, these paths must also account for changing time to expiration and early exercise. A plausible stock path is therefore insufficient: the simulation must assign volatility across strikes and maturities, update it as the path unfolds, and convert each state into an American-option value and Greeks.
The main difficulty is the circular role of market prices and implied volatility (IV) in a common pricing workflow. In observed data, IV is recovered by inverting an option price under a chosen pricing model~\cite{black1973}. A pricer can then use that IV to reproduce the observed price or interpolate nearby contracts. In a forward simulation, however, the future option quote is precisely what is missing. The model must supply a path-conditional IV before it can calculate the synthetic quote. That IV should respond to changing spot, moneyness, and time to expiration without treating an unobserved future option price as an input. The problem is especially clear when the desired stock-return process is empirical and non-Gaussian rather than the return process implied by the option-pricing model.

Existing approaches address different parts of this problem. Parametric surfaces such as SABR and SVI summarize observed volatility smiles with a small number of parameters~\cite{hagan2002,gatheral2004}. Neural surfaces can represent more varied cross-sectional shapes~\cite{horvath2021,bayer2019}. These methods are effective interpolators after they have been fitted, but a fitted surface is static unless the model also specifies how it changes along a simulated path. Local-volatility and stochastic-volatility models instead provide joint dynamics for the stock and its volatility~\cite{dupire1994,derman1994,heston1993}. They offer a coherent route from calibration to pricing, but they also determine the stock dynamics. That coupling is restrictive when the objective is to retain a separate return generator chosen for empirical tail behavior, volatility clustering, or cross-asset dependence.
Return-first methods have the complementary limitation. Historical and generative return models can reproduce non-Gaussian features of equity data, including heavy tails and volatility clustering~\cite{cont2001,wiese2020}. A Jump Hidden Markov Model (JumpHMM), for example, represents returns through persistent latent states and explicit visits to tail states~\cite{jumphmm2025}. Such a generator supplies physical stock paths but does not determine an option-implied volatility surface or an American-option value. Recombining lattices solve the early-exercise problem once spot, maturity, rates, and volatility have been supplied~\cite{cox1979,leisen1996}, but the lattice does not generate the volatility input. Thus the return generator, IV model, and option pricer can each be well specified while the synthetic market remains incomplete.

Taken together, these limitations motivate a modular construction that preserves an empirically chosen return process, evolves IV without future option quotes, and reserves the pricing model for mapping simulated states to American-option values. In the architecture proposed here, market option data enter once, during the offline fit of the cross-sectional IV structure. Information then flows only downstream: the return generator supplies the spot path, the IV module maps current spot, moneyness, and remaining maturity to a contract-specific volatility state, and the lattice consumes spot, IV, strike, maturity, and rates to calculate marks and Greeks. No synthetic quote is fed back into the IV module. This separation resolves the missing-quote circularity while preserving clear roles for evaluation: the return model determines terminal spot and held-to-expiration payoff, the IV module controls interim volatility and risk sensitivities, and the lattice supplies the early-exercise rule. To implement this three-stage framework, we fitted positive neural IV surfaces offline, used JumpHMM to generate stock paths, evolved a separate CIR-like square-root IV factor for each contract around its fitted surface target, and priced the resulting states with Cox--Ross--Rubinstein and Leisen--Reimer lattices. We compared global, sector-specific, and per-ticker surface sharing on 234{,}549 observations from 31 tickers and fifteen dates, and we tested temporal splits with and without earnings-calendar inputs. Fitted Goldman Sachs and Eli Lilly scenarios then demonstrated the full path from simulated returns to IV, option values, Greeks, and terminal profit and loss. These scenarios were computational illustrations rather than out-of-sample trading tests because their surface fits included observations from the same period. More broadly, the framework makes it possible to replace or test the return generator, IV dynamics, or pricer independently while still producing a complete synthetic option market.
