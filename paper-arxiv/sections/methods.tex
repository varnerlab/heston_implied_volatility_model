\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Pipeline and stock-path generation}

The generator passed information through three stages. The complete data flow was:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pipeline}
    \text{JumpHMM stock path}
    \longrightarrow \text{contract IV factor}
    \longrightarrow \text{American lattice price and Greeks}.
\end{equation}
The stages were run forward without lattice feedback. Because market option data entered only during the offline IV-surface fit, a simulated step did not require a contemporaneous option quote. The first stage used pretrained single-ticker marginals from the Jump Hidden Markov Model (JumpHMM), which generates equity returns with heavy tails, weak linear autocorrelation, and persistent volatility clustering~\cite{jumphmm2025,cont2001}. JumpHMM discretizes excess growth rates into latent states with location-scale Student-$t$ emissions, while a jump mechanism increases visits to tail states. A Student-$t$ copula can couple fitted marginals and preserve cross-asset tail dependence~\cite{demarta2005}, although the scenario drivers used here were single-ticker marginals. JumpHMM determined the stock path and therefore the terminal option payoff.

\subsection{Offline IV-surface calibration}

Option-chain data were collected from Alpaca Markets using a free-tier account. The frozen corpus comprised 457 ticker files in fifteen snapshot directories, with 234{,}549 rows after filtering. We retained calls and puts with a positive bid, positive calendar DTE, reported IV strictly between 0.01 and 2.0, and strike/spot between 0.8 and 1.2 inclusive. Spot was the stored underlying-session close. Both option types entered the same IV loss, and repeated contracts across snapshots remained separate observations. We fitted Alpaca's reported IV field rather than reinverting all market mids under our lattice. Alpaca documents a Black--Scholes calculation with a Vega-based iterative IV solver~\cite{alpaca_market_data_faq}. The snapshot directory label, quote-capture timestamp, and underlying-session date were distinct fields; the complete mapping and counts are reported in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:corpus_dates}. In particular, the May 11 underlying-session snapshot was captured on May 12. Temporal earnings splits followed snapshot-directory labels; the May 11 holdout followed the underlying-session date. The corpus supplies extensive cross-sectional observations over a short time window, rather than 234{,}549 independent temporal observations.

The \href{https://github.com/varnerlab/heston_implied_volatility_model}{source repository} provides the experiment drivers and reproduction instructions. The ablation outputs include file hashes, a frozen corpus manifest, pilot constants, path-level marks, numerical audits, and copies of the computational sources. The JumpHMM marginals were trained on equity histories from 2014--2024. The pretrained artifact records 424 ticker marginals, 50 states, 2{,}767 training days, a $1/252$ year step, and zero risk-free offset in the stored growth rates.

SABR and SVI represent IV smiles with low-dimensional parametric functions~\cite{hagan2002,gatheral2004}. Neural representations can fit a broader range of surface shapes~\cite{horvath2021,bayer2019}, but unconstrained networks do not automatically satisfy static no-arbitrage conditions. We compared a five-parameter log-polynomial with global, sector-specific, and per-ticker neural surfaces. Positivity was enforced by exponentiating the network output. Calendar monotonicity and strike convexity were not imposed, so positivity alone did not establish an arbitrage-free surface. For ticker $i$, calendar days to expiration $\tau$, and moneyness $m=K/S$, the parametric comparison used the positive shape function:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi}
 \psi_{\beta}(\tau,m)=\exp\!\left[
 \beta_1\ln\tau+\beta_2\ln m+\beta_3\ln\tau\ln m
 +\beta_4(\ln m)^2+\beta_5(\ln\tau)^2\right].
\end{equation}
The corresponding IV prediction was defined by a ticker level and the surface shape:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_model}
    \widehat\sigma_i^2(\tau,m)=\theta_i\,\psi(\tau,m),
    \qquad \theta_i>0,\quad \psi(\tau,m)>0.
\end{equation}
This expression is also the observation-day calibration target, which we denote by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta_calibration}
    \theta_{\mathrm{cal}}(i,\tau,m)=\theta_i\,\psi(\tau,m).
\end{equation}

The neural models replaced the log-polynomial with a positive network output. For the two-input fits, the surface was:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi_nn}
    \psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau,m;w)
    =\exp\!\left[\mathrm{NN}\!\left(
    \operatorname{std}(\ln\tau),\operatorname{std}(\ln m);w\right)\right].
\end{equation}
Per-ticker fits required at least 2{,}000 observations; other tickers retained sector fits. Inputs were standardized using the training rows. Networks with at least 5{,}000 per-ticker observations, or 2{,}000 pooled group observations, used a $2\to16\to16\to1$ architecture with $\tanh$ activations and 337 parameters. Smaller qualified groups used $2\to8\to8\to1$ with 105 parameters. For the earnings analysis, we appended two standardized inputs: signed days to the ticker's nearest earnings date, clipped to $[-30,30]$, and the minimum absolute earnings distance among same-sector peers, clipped to 30. The resulting $4\to16\to16\to1$ network had 369 parameters. Earnings dates came from the public Yahoo Finance calendar. These event features were evaluated only in the temporal calibration comparison and were not used in the GS and LLY scenario drivers.
Network weights and ticker log levels were fitted jointly by minimizing IV mean squared error. The training objective was:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nn_loss}
 \min_{w,\{\ln\theta_i\}}
 \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
 \left[
 \sqrt{\theta_{i(j)}\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau_j,m_j;w)}
 -\sigma_{\mathrm{market},j}
 \right]^2.
\end{equation}
The log levels were initialized from each ticker's mean squared market IV. Adam training used a decreasing learning-rate schedule, at most 2{,}000 epochs, and a best-training-loss checkpoint with patience 200. Because a constant can be shifted between $\ln\theta_i$ and the output bias of the network, these two components are not separately identified. Only their product in~\eqref{eq:sigma_model} is interpretable without an additional normalization constraint.

\subsection{Contract-specific dynamic IV factors}\label{sec:theta_function}

The standard Heston model couples an asset-price diffusion to a square-root instantaneous-variance process~\cite{heston1993}. Its variance equation is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heston_standard}
    dv_t = \kappa(\theta-v_t)\,dt + \sigma_v\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^v .
\end{equation}
Here $\kappa$ is the mean-reversion rate, $\theta$ is long-run variance, and $\sigma_v$ is volatility of variance. In the standard model, the Brownian motion in~\eqref{eq:heston_standard} is correlated with the Brownian motion that drives the stock. Our use of this process was narrower: we retained positive square-root fluctuations and mean reversion, but JumpHMM generated the stock separately, and we evolved one variance state for each contract rather than one instantaneous variance state for the underlying. The process therefore supplied a time-varying diffusion-volatility input to a constant-volatility lattice at each simulated date. It should be interpreted as a CIR-like dynamic IV factor, not as the instantaneous return variance of a standard Heston economy.

Each scenario contract $a=(i,K,T)$ received a separate variance state $v_{a,t}$. Its moving mean-reversion target used the fitted surface at the current spot and remaining calendar DTE. The target was:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta}
    \bar v_{a,t}=\theta_i\,
    \psi_{\mathrm{NN}}\!\left(\tau_t,K/S_t\right).
\end{equation}
The factor started exactly on the fitted entry surface:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:v0}
    v_{a,0}=\bar v_{a,0}.
\end{equation}
At each later date, the lattice IV was the square root of that contract's factor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_path}
    \sigma_{a,t}=\sqrt{v_{a,t}}.
\end{equation}
Thus a put and call on the same stock shared the stock path and variance shock but retained different variance states and different surface targets.
To couple these factors to the return paths, the JumpHMM advanced only on exchange sessions. We shifted every simulated growth-rate observation by a constant so the ensemble mean matched a prespecified annual growth prior. We then standardized the one-session simulated log returns across all paths and dates. The return shock used in the IV update was:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:return_shock}
    Z^S_{j,t}=\frac{\Delta\ln S_{j,t}-\overline{\Delta\ln S}}
    {s_{\Delta\ln S}}.
\end{equation}
An independent standard normal shock supplied the remaining variation. The coupled factor shock was:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variance_shock}
    Z^v_{j,t}=\rho Z^S_{j,t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,Z^{\perp}_{j,t},
    \qquad Z^{\perp}_{j,t}\sim\mathcal N(0,1).
\end{equation}
The coefficient $\rho$ was fixed at $-0.6$. It was a return-shock coupling parameter, not a calibrated correlation between two Brownian motions in a standard Heston model.
We then used a hard-floor Euler update with a trading-year step $\Delta t=1/252$. The update was:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler}
 v_{a,t+1}=\max\!\left\{
 v_{a,t}+\kappa(\bar v_{a,t}-v_{a,t})\Delta t
 +\sigma_v\sqrt{\max(v_{a,t},0)}\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z^v_{j,t},
 v_{\min}\right\}.
\end{equation}
We fixed $\kappa=15$, $\sigma_v=0.5$, and $v_{\min}=(0.005)^2$. These values were scenario assumptions, not estimates from the option corpus. The floor protected the numerical pricer when the parameters did not satisfy the Feller condition~\cite{andersen2008}. Although the repository also contains a proposed state-dependent level and aggregate tail-state mood multiplier, these terms remained inactive in every reported experiment. The results therefore evaluate only the fitted level-times-surface model and fixed return-shock coupling. Evaluating state- and mood-dependent IV would require state-conditioned option histories and remained outside this study.

\subsection{American-option pricing, calendar clock, and terminal profit and loss}

Recombining lattices price American options by backward induction and compare continuation with immediate exercise at every node~\cite{broadie1996}. The Cox--Ross--Rubinstein construction uses up and down factors based on the supplied volatility~\cite{cox1979}. Jarrow--Rudd and Tian use alternative moment matches~\cite{jarrow1983,tian1993}. Leisen--Reimer uses a Peizer--Pratt inversion that places the strike more consistently in the tree and improves finite-step convergence~\cite{leisen1996}. Both pricers used here accepted one IV value for a given contract and date; they did not generate the IV surface themselves. The GS and LLY contracts ran from April 28 to May 29, 2026, an interval with 31 calendar days but only 22 exchange-session transitions after excluding weekends and the May 25 market holiday. Calendar DTE entered the surface and lattice, whereas JumpHMM and~\eqref{eq:euler} advanced on the 22 trading transitions. For a date with $\tau_t$ calendar days remaining, the price input used $\tau_t/365$ years.

Corpus-wide dollar-price checks used a 200-step CRR American tree. Scenario marks and Greeks used a 201-step Leisen-Reimer American tree. Both stages used a constant risk-free rate of $4.25\%$ and continuous dividend yield $q=0$. At expiration, the model replaced the lattice value with intrinsic value. For a short position that received market mid $p_0$, terminal profit and loss was:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:terminal_pnl}
 \Pi_T^{\mathrm{put}}=p_0^{\mathrm{put}}-\max(K-S_T,0),
 \qquad
 \Pi_T^{\mathrm{call}}=p_0^{\mathrm{call}}-\max(S_T-K,0).
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:terminal_pnl} shows that terminal profit and loss depended on the JumpHMM terminal stock price, strike, and premium. Dynamic IV affected interim option marks and Greeks, but not a held-to-expiration payoff. All option premiums, values, and P\&L are dollars per underlying share; monetary amounts for a standard 100-share contract are 100 times these values. Premium financing was omitted. For low-volatility states or saturated Leisen--Reimer probabilities, an American CRR tree supplied the mark, retaining the immediate-exercise comparison.
Greeks were calculated by central finite differences on the same Leisen-Reimer pricer. Spot bumps were $\pm1.5\%$ of spot for delta and gamma. Volatility bumps were $\pm0.005$, and Vega was reported per one volatility percentage point. We assumed execution at the observed mid, no transaction costs, no margin constraint, no early assignment before the plotted horizon, and no discrete dividends.

\subsection{Paired dynamic IV ablation}\label{sec:ablation_methods}

We tested which IV components changed interim valuation by holding the stock paths, contracts, entry premiums, and pricing assumptions fixed across five variants. Frozen IV retained each contract's fitted entry value of $v$. The direct surface set $v_{a,t}=\bar v_{a,t}$ at every date. Deterministic mean reversion used~\eqref{eq:euler} with $\sigma_v=0$. An uncoupled stochastic factor used $\sigma_v=0.5$ and $\rho=0$, and the full factor used $\sigma_v=0.5$ and $\rho=-0.6$. The last three variants retained $\kappa=15$. All variants started at the same fitted entry IV; every stochastic variant reused the same independent normal innovation across contracts and variants at each path and date.

Each ticker used three evaluation seeds, 20260429, 20260430, and 20260431, with 1{,}000 paths per seed. For this ablation, a separate 10{,}000-path pilot with seed 20260905 supplied a constant growth shift to the 10\% annual prior and the resulting one-session log-return mean and standard deviation. Those constants were frozen before generating evaluation paths. Unlike the original fitted illustrations, evaluation paths were therefore not recentered or standardized using their own future realizations. The pilot and evaluation simulations each used 22 transitions, and every return was applied once after the initial stock price.

The primary endpoint was liquidation after ten trading transitions, on May 12, with 17 calendar days remaining. Secondary endpoints used five, fifteen, and twenty transitions. The short-position liquidation P\&L and its paired difference from the direct-surface reference were defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ablation_pnl}
    \Pi_{a,t}^{(b,j)}=p_{a,0}-P_{a,t}^{(b,j)},
    \qquad D_{a,t}^{(b,j)}=\Pi_{a,t}^{(b,j)}-\Pi_{a,t}^{(\mathrm{surface},j)}.
\end{equation}
Here $b$ denotes the IV variant and $j$ the shared stock path. We reported mean P\&L, its standard deviation, the 5\% quantile, and 5\% expected shortfall, defined as mean P\&L at or below that quantile. The paired mean difference had Monte Carlo standard error $s_D/\sqrt{n}$. Mean absolute mark differences measured the size of pathwise changes even when positive and negative differences canceled in the mean. We reported each seed separately and pooled 3{,}000 paths per ticker. These uncertainty estimates condition on the fitted models and pilot constants. Identical entry marks and terminal payoffs were checked exactly across variants.

Numerical checks compared 201- and 401-step marks on the first twenty paths of every evaluation seed at transitions ten and twenty. A separate audit used eleven fixed strikes from 0.8 to 1.2 times entry spot, the first fifty paths of the first seed, and transitions zero, ten, and twenty. Calls and puts at the scenario expiry shared the same shocks. We checked intrinsic and upper price bounds, strike monotonicity, vertical-spread bounds, and convexity at both tree depths, counting violations larger than \$0.01 per share. Convexity violations were measured as the dollar excess above the neighboring linear interpolation. Entry was counted once per variant and option type. These are necessary strike conditions; the audit did not test calendar or dynamic no-arbitrage.
