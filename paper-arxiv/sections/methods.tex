\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Simulator overview and stock paths}

The simulator worked in two phases. Before any simulation, we fitted IV surfaces to observed option quotes, and each surface predicted an option's IV from its moneyness and time to expiration. The simulation itself used only these fitted surfaces and needed no further option quotes. It first generated complete stock paths from JumpHMM and then moved forward along each path one trading day at a time. On each day, a binomial tree priced each option from the current stock price, the remaining time to expiration, and the option's current IV, and the same tree supplied its price sensitivities (Greeks). The option's implied variance then moved partway toward the variance that the fitted surface predicted for its current moneyness and remaining time, with random shocks linked to the stock's return. Option prices never fed back into the surfaces or the stock paths, so information flowed in one direction, from the stock price to IV to the option price (Algorithm~\ref{alg:forward_sim}).

JumpHMM supplied the stock paths~\cite{jumphmm2025}. We used a pretrained version fitted separately to each ticker's price history from 2014 to 2024. The model describes each ticker's excess growth rate with hidden states, and each state draws the growth rate from its own location-scale Student-$t$ distribution, called the state's emission distribution. A jump mechanism increases visits to tail states. In the original study, this reproduced heavy tails and part of the volatility clustering of equity returns~\cite{jumphmm2025}, two features documented across many markets~\cite{cont2001}. The pretrained model covered 424 tickers, each with 50 states fitted on 2{,}767 trading days. Its growth rates used a time step of $1/252$ year and a risk-free offset of zero. A Student-$t$ copula can link the returns of several tickers~\cite{demarta2005}, but every scenario reported here simulated one ticker at a time.

For price simulation, we cut off each Student-$t$ emission distribution at a finite bound. The simulated excess growth rate in state $k$ was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:truncated_emission}
 G_t=\mu_k+\sigma_k Z_t,\qquad
 Z_t\sim t_{\nu_k}\mid |Z_t|\le b,\qquad b=10,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_k$ and $\sigma_k$ are the fitted location and scale of state $k$, $\nu_k$ is its degrees of freedom, and the cutoff $b$ is measured in multiples of the scale $\sigma_k$. We applied the cutoff only in simulation, and the locations, scales, transition matrix, and jump parameters kept their fitted values. We redrew any value outside the bound before adding the constant drift adjustment (growth shift) and converting log returns to prices, so no stock path was discarded. The cutoff kept the states and the jump mechanism intact and gave the stock price a finite mean and variance at every horizon. Without it, exponentiating Student-$t$ log returns gives an infinite expected stock price, so mean call values and expected short-call losses would have no finite population value~\cite{cassidy2009}. To examine sensitivity, we repeated the simulations with a wider cutoff, $b=20$, using the same contracts, seeds, and evaluation dates and recomputing the normalization constants for each cutoff. Neither cutoff was chosen by forecast performance (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:emission_bounds}, \ref{tab:tail_sensitivity}, and~\ref{tab:tail_forecasts}).

\subsection{Option data and IV surfaces}

We downloaded option-chain snapshots daily from Alpaca Markets with a free account. This account supplies Alpaca's indicative feed, a modified version of the exchange (OPRA) feed~\cite{alpaca_option_feed}. Every quote, midpoint, spread, and reported IV in this study is therefore an Alpaca value, which may differ from exchange quotes and from prices at which trades could execute. The calibration data set contained 457 ticker files from fifteen daily snapshots, and 234{,}549 observations remained after filtering. The filters kept calls and puts with a positive bid, positive calendar days to expiration (DTE), reported IV strictly between 0.01 and 2.0, and strike/spot between 0.8 and 1.2 inclusive. Spot was the stored closing price of the underlying for that session. Calls and puts entered the same loss, and a contract that appeared in several snapshots counted once per snapshot. The fitting target was the IV that Alpaca reports with each quote. Alpaca documents this IV as a Black--Scholes calculation solved iteratively using the price sensitivity to volatility (Vega)~\cite{alpaca_market_data_faq}. The April earnings holdout split dates by snapshot label, and the other temporal tests split by underlying-session date (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:corpus_dates}).

The data covered many tickers, strikes, and expirations but only a short window of dates. We used them to compare a five-parameter log-polynomial surface with neural surfaces shared by all tickers, shared within a sector, or fitted to a single ticker. The log-polynomial follows the compact parametric tradition of SABR and SVI~\cite{hagan2002,gatheral2004}, and the neural surfaces allow more flexible shapes. We exponentiated each shape function to keep it positive. We imposed neither calendar monotonicity nor strike convexity during fitting, so the fitted surfaces needed separate no-arbitrage checks. The parametric shape was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi}
 \psi_{\beta}(\tau,m)=\exp\!\left[
 \beta_1\ln\tau+\beta_2\ln m+\beta_3\ln\tau\ln m
 +\beta_4(\ln m)^2+\beta_5(\ln\tau)^2\right],
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is calendar DTE, $m=K/S$ is moneyness, and $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_5$ are fitted coefficients. The predicted squared IV for ticker $i$ multiplied a ticker level by the shape:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_model}
    \widehat\sigma_i^2(\tau,m)=\theta_i\,\psi(\tau,m),
    \qquad \theta_i>0,\quad \psi(\tau,m)>0,
\end{equation}
where $\theta_i$ sets the overall IV level of ticker $i$ and $\psi$ sets how IV varies with moneyness and maturity. The same product, evaluated at an option's current moneyness and maturity, later served as the variance target in the simulation.

The neural surfaces replaced the log-polynomial with the exponential of a neural network (NN) output, given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi_nn}
    \psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau,m;w)
    =\exp\!\left[\mathrm{NN}\!\left(
    \operatorname{std}(\ln\tau),\operatorname{std}(\ln m);w\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $w$ denotes the network weights and $\operatorname{std}(\cdot)$ standardizes each input with the mean and standard deviation of the training rows. A ticker received its own network only if it had at least 2{,}000 observations. Tickers below that threshold used their sector's network. Networks fitted to at least 5{,}000 observations for one ticker, or at least 2{,}000 observations for a pooled group, had two hidden layers of 16 $\tanh$ units ($2\to16\to16\to1$, 337 parameters). Smaller qualifying fits used two hidden layers of 8 units ($2\to8\to8\to1$, 105 parameters). For the earnings analysis, we added two standardized inputs. The first was the signed number of days to the ticker's nearest earnings date, clipped to $[-30,30]$. The second was the smallest absolute number of days to an earnings date among the ticker's sector peers, clipped to 30. For the three ETFs, both inputs were the smallest absolute number of days to an earnings date among all 28 non-ETF stocks. The resulting network had 369 parameters ($4\to16\to16\to1$). Earnings dates came from the public Yahoo Finance calendar. These inputs entered only the April holdout comparison and a later refit of the July surfaces that tested the earnings explanation, and the GS and LLY simulations never used them. We fitted the network weights and the ticker log levels jointly by minimizing the mean squared difference between predicted and reported IV, which weights every retained observation equally:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nn_loss}
 \min_{w,\{\ln\theta_i\}}
 \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
 \left[
 \sqrt{\theta_{i(j)}\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau_j,m_j;w)}
 -\sigma_{\mathrm{market},j}
 \right]^2,
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of observations, $i(j)$ is the ticker of observation $j$, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{market},j}$ is its reported IV. We initialized each log level as the logarithm of the squared mean reported IV for that ticker. Training used the Adam optimizer with a decreasing learning rate and at most 2{,}000 epochs. It stopped after 200 epochs without improvement in training loss, and we kept the weights from the final epoch. A constant can move between $\ln\theta_i$ and the network's output bias without changing the fit. The two are therefore not separately identified, and only their product in~\eqref{eq:sigma_model} can be interpreted without an extra normalization.

\subsection{Daily IV updates}\label{sec:theta_function}

The standard Heston model pairs a stock-price diffusion with a square-root process for the stock's instantaneous variance~\cite{heston1993}. That variance follows the stochastic differential equation given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heston_standard}
    dv_t = \kappa(\theta-v_t)\,dt + \sigma_v\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^v ,
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the mean-reversion rate, $\theta$ is the long-run variance, $\sigma_v$ is the volatility of variance, and $W_t^v$ is a Brownian motion that the standard model correlates with the one driving the stock. We used the same square-root equation for a different quantity. Each option received its own implied-variance state, which we call its IV factor, and that factor evolved along a stock path supplied by JumpHMM. Each factor reverted toward a target read from the fitted surface, and its square root was the volatility input to that option's binomial tree. Each pricing calculation held that volatility constant, and the factor changed only between simulated dates. The factor is therefore the implied variance of one option, not the instantaneous variance of the stock's returns. For an option $a=(i,K,T)$ on ticker $i$ with strike $K$ and expiration $T$, the factor $v_{a,t}$ reverted toward the target given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta}
    \bar v_{a,t}=\theta_i\,
    \psi_{\mathrm{NN}}\!\left(\tau_t,K/S_t\right),
\end{equation}
where $S_t$ is the stock price and $\tau_t$ the remaining calendar DTE on date $t$, so the target moved as the stock price and the time to expiration changed. Each factor started on the fitted surface at entry,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:v0}
    v_{a,0}=\bar v_{a,0},
\end{equation}
and on every date the IV entering the binomial tree was its square root:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_path}
    \sigma_{a,t}=\sqrt{v_{a,t}}.
\end{equation}

A put and a call on the same stock shared its path and variance shock but kept separate factors and targets. The stock and the factors advanced only on exchange trading days. In the original GS and LLY illustrations, we added a constant to every simulated growth rate so that the average across paths matched a prespecified annual growth prior. We call this constant the growth shift. We then standardized the one-session log returns across all paths and dates. The comparison of five IV variants instead estimated both constants from a separate pilot simulation. The standardized return shock entering the IV update was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:return_shock}
    Z^S_{j,t}=\frac{\Delta\ln S_{j,t}-\overline{\Delta\ln S}}
    {s_{\Delta\ln S}},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\ln S_{j,t}$ is the one-session log return on path $j$ at date $t$, and $\overline{\Delta\ln S}$ and $s_{\Delta\ln S}$ are its mean and standard deviation across paths and dates. The shock to each factor combined this return shock with an independent standard normal shock:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variance_shock}
    Z^v_{j,t}=\rho Z^S_{j,t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,Z^{\perp}_{j,t},
    \qquad Z^{\perp}_{j,t}\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ sets how strongly the factor responds to the stock's return. We fixed $\rho=-0.6$, so IV tended to rise when the stock fell. This $\rho$ is a chosen weight on the return shock, not a correlation estimated from data. Each factor advanced once per trading day by an Euler-type step of the square-root equation, with $\Delta t=1/252$ and a floor at $v_{\min}$. The step used $Z^v_{j,t}$ in place of a Brownian increment, so its shocks were not exactly normal:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler}
 v_{a,t+1}=\max\!\left\{
 v_{a,t}+\kappa(\bar v_{a,t}-v_{a,t})\Delta t
 +\sigma_v\sqrt{\max(v_{a,t},0)}\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z^v_{j,t},
 v_{\min}\right\}.
\end{equation}
We fixed $\kappa=15$, $\sigma_v=0.5$, and $v_{\min}=(0.005)^2$ as scenario assumptions. The floor kept the factor positive for the pricer, because an Euler step can fall below zero whether or not these values satisfy the Feller condition~\cite{andersen2008}. The code also contains two proposed extensions, a target level that depends on JumpHMM's hidden state and a multiplier driven by visits to tail states. Both were switched off in every reported experiment, so the fitted surface alone set the target and the fixed $\rho$ alone linked IV to stock returns.

\subsection{American-option pricing and profit and loss}

A binomial tree (lattice) prices an American option by working backward from expiration and taking, at every node, the larger of the value of continuing and the value of exercising immediately~\cite{cox1979,broadie1996}. The Cox--Ross--Rubinstein (CRR) tree sets its up and down moves from the supplied volatility~\cite{cox1979}. Alternatives include the moment-matching trees of Jarrow--Rudd and Tian~\cite{jarrow1983,tian1993} and the Leisen--Reimer tree, which uses the Peizer--Pratt inversion to converge faster as steps are added~\cite{leisen1996}. Both trees used here took one IV value per option and date. The GS and LLY options ran from April 28 to May 29, 2026, a span of 31 calendar days and 22 trading-day steps after excluding weekends and the May 25 market holiday. The surface and the tree measured time to expiration in calendar days, with $\tau_t/365$ years entering the pricer, while the stock and the factors advanced on the 22 trading-day steps. Dollar-price checks across the calibration data used a 200-step American CRR tree. Scenario prices and Greeks used a 201-step American Leisen--Reimer (LR) tree. Both used a constant risk-free rate of $4.25\%$ and a continuous dividend yield $q=0$. In low-volatility states, or when the Leisen--Reimer probabilities saturated, an American CRR tree priced the option instead and kept the early-exercise comparison. On each trading day, the simulation priced each option from its current stock price, remaining maturity, and factor, and then advanced the factor toward its moving target. At expiration, the option's intrinsic value replaced the tree price (Algorithm~\ref{alg:forward_sim}).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Forward simulation of contract values}\label{alg:forward_sim}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require JumpHMM model for ticker $i$; fitted $(\theta_i,\psi_i)$; contract $(K,T)$; trading dates $d_0,\ldots,d_L$; calendar DTE $\tau_0,\ldots,\tau_L$; $(\kappa,\sigma_v,\rho)$; $S_0$; $r$; $N_p$ paths; LR tree steps $N_{\mathrm{LR}}$.
\Ensure Stock, variance, IV, and option-value paths.
\State Draw $N_p$ JumpHMM stock paths with $L$ trading transitions and emissions truncated as in~\eqref{eq:truncated_emission}.
\State Compute $Z^S_{j,\ell}$ by standardizing all simulated one-session log returns as in~\eqref{eq:return_shock}.
\State Draw $Z^\perp_{j,\ell}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ once per path and transition; set $Z^v_{j,\ell}\gets\rho Z^S_{j,\ell}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}Z^\perp_{j,\ell}$ and reuse it for every contract.
\For{contract $a$ and path $j$}
  \State $v_{a,0}^{(j)} \gets \theta_i\psi_i(\tau_0,K/S_0)$
  \For{$\ell=0$ \textbf{to} $L-1$}
    \State $\sigma_{a,\ell}^{(j)} \gets \sqrt{v_{a,\ell}^{(j)}}$
    \State $P_{a,\ell}^{(j)} \gets \mathrm{LR}_{N_{\mathrm{LR}}}(S_{j,\ell},K,\sigma_{a,\ell}^{(j)},\tau_\ell/365,r)$
    \State $\bar v_{a,\ell}^{(j)}\gets\theta_i\psi_i(\tau_\ell,K/S_{j,\ell})$
    \State Update $v_{a,\ell+1}^{(j)}$ with the floored Euler step in~\eqref{eq:euler} using $\Delta t=1/252$.
  \EndFor
  \State $P_{a,L}^{(j)}\gets\max\{\phi(S_{j,L}-K),0\}$, where $\phi=1$ for a call and $\phi=-1$ for a put.
\EndFor
\State \Return all paths.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For a short position that sold the option at the captured indicative midpoint $p_0$, profit and loss at expiration was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:terminal_pnl}
 \Pi_T^{\mathrm{put}}=p_0^{\mathrm{put}}-\max(K-S_T,0),
 \qquad
 \Pi_T^{\mathrm{call}}=p_0^{\mathrm{call}}-\max(S_T-K,0),
\end{equation}
where $S_T$ is the JumpHMM stock price at expiration. This P\&L depended only on $S_T$, the strike, and the premium, so the IV updates changed option prices and Greeks before expiration but not the payoff of a position held to expiration. All premiums, option values, and P\&L are in dollars per share, so amounts for a standard 100-share contract are 100 times these values. We calculated Greeks by central finite differences on the same Leisen--Reimer tree. Delta measures the price's sensitivity to the stock price, and gamma measures how delta changes with the stock price. Delta and gamma used stock-price bumps of $\pm1.5\%$, and Vega used volatility bumps of $\pm0.005$ and is reported per volatility percentage point. We assumed execution at the observed mid, no transaction costs, no margin constraint, no premium financing, no early assignment before the plotted horizon, and no discrete dividends.

\subsection{Comparison of five IV variants}\label{sec:ablation_methods}

To find which parts of the IV update changed option values before expiration, we compared five IV variants on the same stock paths, options, entry premiums, and pricing assumptions. Frozen IV kept each option's IV at its fitted entry value. The direct surface set the factor to the surface target, $v_{a,t}=\bar v_{a,t}$, on every date. Deterministic mean reversion used the update in~\eqref{eq:euler} without shocks ($\sigma_v=0$). The uncoupled stochastic factor added shocks unrelated to stock returns ($\sigma_v=0.5$, $\rho=0$). The full factor, also called the coupled factor, linked those shocks to stock returns ($\sigma_v=0.5$, $\rho=-0.6$). All three variants that evolved used $\kappa=15$. Every variant started from the same fitted entry IV, and the two stochastic variants reused the same independent normal shocks. We ran this paired comparison, which we call the IV ablation, with three seeds per ticker (20260429, 20260430, and 20260431) and 1{,}000 paths per seed. A separate 10{,}000-path pilot simulation (seed 20260905) supplied the growth shift toward the 10\% annual prior and the mean and standard deviation of one-session log returns. We fixed these constants before generating the evaluation paths. Unlike the original illustrations, the evaluation paths were therefore never recentered or standardized with their own future values. The pilot and evaluation simulations each used 22 trading-day steps, and each simulated return was applied exactly once after the initial stock price.

The primary endpoint closed the position after ten trading days, on May 12 with 17 calendar days to expiration. Secondary endpoints closed it after five, fifteen, and twenty trading days. The P\&L from closing a short position early, and its difference from the direct surface on the same path, were given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ablation_pnl}
    \Pi_{a,t}^{(b,j)}=p_{a,0}-P_{a,t}^{(b,j)},
    \qquad D_{a,t}^{(b,j)}=\Pi_{a,t}^{(b,j)}-\Pi_{a,t}^{(\mathrm{surface},j)},
\end{equation}
where $b$ denotes the IV variant, $j$ the shared stock path, and $P_{a,t}^{(b,j)}$ the option's price on date $t$. We reported mean P\&L, its standard deviation, the 5\% quantile, and 5\% expected shortfall, defined as mean P\&L at or below that quantile. The Monte Carlo standard error of the mean paired difference was $s_D/\sqrt{n}$, where $s_D$ is the standard deviation of $D$ across $n$ paths. Mean absolute price differences measured the size of the changes on individual paths, which can cancel in the mean. We reported each seed separately and also pooled the 3{,}000 paths per ticker. These standard errors measure simulation noise with the fitted models and pilot constants held fixed.

We also checked that entry prices and payoffs at expiration agreed exactly across variants, and we examined the numerical accuracy and strike consistency of the prices in between. The numerical check compared 201-step and 401-step prices on the first twenty paths of every evaluation seed after ten and twenty trading days. A separate check across strikes (the strike audit) priced calls and puts at eleven fixed strikes from 0.8 to 1.2 times the entry stock price, all with the scenario expiration and the same shocks. It used the first fifty paths of the first seed at entry and after ten and twenty trading days. At both tree depths, we checked intrinsic-value and upper price bounds, monotonicity in strike, vertical-spread bounds, and convexity in strike. A convexity violation was the dollar amount by which a price exceeded the straight line between its neighbors, and we counted violations larger than \$0.01 per share. Entry prices were counted once per variant and option type. Passing these checks is necessary for prices to be consistent across strikes. The audit compared strikes within one expiration date, so consistency across expiration dates and over time remained untested.
