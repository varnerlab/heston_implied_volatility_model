\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Simulator overview and stock paths}

The simulator values options along simulated stock paths in two phases. In the first phase, before any simulation, we fitted IV surfaces to observed option quotes. Each surface predicts an option's IV from its moneyness (strike divided by stock price) and its time to expiration. In the second phase, the simulator generates complete stock paths and then moves forward along each path one trading day at a time, using the fitted surfaces and, apart from the starting IVs in the forecast test, no further option quotes. On each day, a binomial tree prices each option from the current stock price, the remaining time to expiration, and the option's current IV. The same tree supplies the option's price sensitivities (Greeks). The option's implied variance, the square of its IV, then moves partway toward the variance that the surface predicts for its current moneyness and remaining time. The move also carries random shocks partly tied to the stock's return. Option prices never feed back into the surfaces or the stock paths, so information flows in one direction, from the stock price to IV to the option price (Algorithm~\ref{alg:forward_sim}).

We used the fitted surfaces and the simulator in five analyses. First, we measured how closely the surfaces matched the quotes they were fitted to, in IV and in dollar prices, and how well they transferred to dates withheld from fitting. Second, we simulated short positions in one put and one call on Goldman Sachs (GS) and on Eli Lilly (LLY), opened on April 28, 2026, as illustrations. Third, we repriced these options on new simulated paths under five ways of setting IV: holding it fixed, setting it each day to the surface's prediction, and three versions of the daily update described below. All five shared the same stock paths, so their differences isolate the effect of each rule, and we call this paired comparison the IV ablation. Fourth, we tested the surfaces and the simulator on dates after the calibration window, which ended on May 11, including a forecast test of GS and LLY option prices issued daily in August and early September. Fifth, we compared the stock forecasts of JumpHMM, the stock model described next, with those of simpler stock models.

The stock paths came from the Jump Hidden Markov Model (JumpHMM)~\cite{jumphmm2025}. We used the per-ticker models fitted in the study that introduced JumpHMM, which fitted 424 tickers separately, each on 2{,}767 trading days of prices from 2014 to 2024. Each model describes a ticker's excess growth rate, its annualized log return minus a risk-free rate, with 50 hidden states. Each state draws the growth rate from its own location-scale Student-$t$ distribution, called the state's emission distribution. The tail states are the few states at each end of the growth-rate range, which produce extreme growth rates. A jump mechanism occasionally forces the model into a tail state and keeps it there for a run of consecutive days, called a jump episode, whose length follows a Poisson distribution. The original study tuned how often episodes start and how long they last. In the original study, the model reproduced heavy tails and part of the volatility clustering of equity returns~\cite{jumphmm2025}, two features documented across many markets~\cite{cont2001}. The models used a time step of $1/252$ year and a risk-free rate of zero, so each growth rate is simply an annualized log return. Through a Student-$t$ copula~\cite{demarta2005}, JumpHMM can link several tickers, but every simulation here used one ticker at a time.

For price simulation, we cut off each Student-$t$ emission distribution at a finite bound. Without a cutoff, exponentiating Student-$t$ log returns gives an infinite expected stock price, so mean call values and expected short-call losses would have no finite population value~\cite{cassidy2009}. The simulated growth rate on day $t$ was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:truncated_emission}
 G_t=\mu_{k_t}+\sigma_{k_t} Z_t,\qquad
 Z_t\sim t_{\nu_{k_t}}\mid |Z_t|\le b,\qquad b=10,
\end{equation}
where $k_t$ is the hidden state on day $t$, $\mu_k$ and $\sigma_k$ are the fitted location and scale of state $k$, $\nu_k$ is its degrees of freedom, and $Z_t$ is a Student-$t$ draw restricted to $|Z_t|\le b$. The cutoff $b$ is therefore measured in multiples of the state's scale. We redrew any value outside the bound, so no stock path was discarded. The cutoff kept the states and the jump mechanism intact and gave the stock price a finite mean and variance at every horizon. We applied it only in simulation, and the locations, scales, transition matrix, and jump parameters kept their fitted values. Each simulated price then followed from $S_{t+1}=S_t\exp[(G_t+c)\Delta t]$ with $\Delta t=1/252$, where the growth shift $c$ is a constant added to every growth rate. We set $c$ separately for each ticker so that the mean simulated growth rate, an annualized log return, equaled 10\% a year. The illustrations estimated $c$ from their own simulated paths. The IV ablation and the forecast test instead estimated it from a separate pilot simulation and then drew fresh paths for scoring, which we call evaluation paths, so the scored paths never influenced $c$. Without this shift, the fitted models would carry the average drift of their 2014--2024 fitting period into every simulation. We set the cutoff $b=10$, and a wider $b=20$ for a sensitivity check, in advance as stress assumptions rather than by forecast performance. The sensitivity reruns repeated each analysis with the same options, seeds, and dates. For $b=20$, they recomputed the growth shift and the other constants estimated from simulated returns (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:emission_bounds}, \ref{tab:tail_sensitivity}, and~\ref{tab:tail_forecasts}).

\subsection{Option data and IV surfaces}

We downloaded option data from Alpaca Markets with a free account, which supplies Alpaca's indicative feed. Alpaca derives this feed from the consolidated exchange feed of the Options Price Reporting Authority (OPRA), but its quotes are indicative rather than actual exchange quotes~\cite{alpaca_option_feed}. Every quote, midpoint, spread, and reported IV in this study is therefore an Alpaca value, which may differ from exchange quotes and from prices at which trades could execute. After each trading session, a download script saved one file per ticker with the session's closing stock price and the quoted calls and puts. Each file kept only the expirations nearest a fixed ladder of target maturities, 2, 7, 14, 30, and 45 calendar days, with 60 and 90 days added on some dates. The data therefore covered many strikes but only a few expirations per ticker and day. The fitting target was the IV that Alpaca reports with each quote. Alpaca computes this IV with the Black--Scholes formula, solved iteratively using the price sensitivity to volatility (Vega)~\cite{alpaca_market_data_faq}. Because that formula prices European options, an American tree given this IV need not reproduce the quoted price exactly.

Each daily download is a snapshot. It has three dates, the label of its data folder, the date the files were downloaded, and the session whose closing price they store. These usually agree but not always (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:corpus_dates}). Some downloads ran after midnight UTC, so the snapshot labeled April 14 stores the April 13 close, and one folder, labeled April 29, holds a download of the April 30 close. For the dates used most below, April 28 and May 11, the label and the session agree. The calibration data set used the first fifteen snapshots, labeled April 14 through May 11, 2026. Not every trading day in that span was captured. It contained 457 ticker files, because the first snapshot covered 23 tickers and each later snapshot covered 31. The 31 tickers were 28 stocks and three exchange-traded funds (ETFs). After filtering, 234{,}549 observations remained. The filters kept calls and puts with a positive bid, positive calendar days to expiration (DTE), reported IV strictly between 0.01 and 2.0, and moneyness $K/S$ between 0.8 and 1.2 inclusive, with $S$ the stored closing stock price. The calibration fits used the DTE stored in each file, which in two snapshots, labeled April 23 and May 1, counted from the download date rather than the session date. Calls and puts entered the same fitting loss, and an option that appeared in several snapshots counted once per snapshot. The download script continued daily through September 4, 2026. We used these later files to refit sector networks on data through July 31 and to score surfaces on later dates.

We compared four ways of sharing surfaces across tickers: one parametric surface for all tickers, one neural surface for all tickers, one neural surface per sector, and neural surfaces for single tickers. The six sectors group the 31 tickers by industry, with the three ETFs as a sector of their own (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:sector_map}). Every surface predicts squared IV as a ticker level multiplied by a shape function of maturity and moneyness. Each shape is the exponential of a polynomial or of a network output, which keeps it positive. Two conditions rule out simple arbitrage. Calendar monotonicity requires total implied variance, squared IV times time to expiration, never to fall as maturity lengthens at fixed moneyness. Strike convexity requires option prices to be convex in strike. We imposed neither condition during fitting, so the fitted surfaces needed separate no-arbitrage checks. The parametric shape follows the compact tradition of SABR and SVI~\cite{hagan2002,gatheral2004}, and all tickers shared its coefficients. It was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi}
 \psi_{\beta}(\tau,m)=\exp\!\left[
 \beta_1\ln\tau+\beta_2\ln m+\beta_3\ln\tau\ln m
 +\beta_4(\ln m)^2+\beta_5(\ln\tau)^2\right],
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is calendar DTE in days, $m=K/S$ is moneyness with strike $K$ and stock price $S$, and $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_5$ are fitted coefficients. The predicted squared IV for ticker $i$ was then given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_model}
    \widehat\sigma_i^2(\tau,m)=\theta_i\,\psi(\tau,m),
    \qquad \theta_i>0,\quad \psi(\tau,m)>0,
\end{equation}
where $\theta_i$ sets the overall IV level of ticker $i$ and $\psi$ is either the parametric shape or a neural shape. The parametric fit estimated the shared coefficients and the ticker levels together by minimizing the same IV loss as the networks.

The neural surfaces replaced the polynomial with the output of a neural network (NN), given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi_nn}
    \psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau,m;w)
    =\exp\!\left[\mathrm{NN}\!\left(
    \operatorname{std}(\ln\tau),\operatorname{std}(\ln m);w\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $w$ denotes the network weights and $\operatorname{std}(\cdot)$ standardizes each input with the mean and standard deviation of the training rows. The global network pooled all tickers, and each sector network pooled the tickers of one sector. A ticker received its own network only if it had at least 2{,}000 observations, and tickers below that threshold kept their sector's network. The global and sector networks all had two hidden layers of 16 $\tanh$ units ($2\to16\to16\to1$, 337 parameters). A single-ticker network had the same size if its ticker had at least 5{,}000 observations and two hidden layers of 8 units ($2\to8\to8\to1$, 105 parameters) otherwise. We fitted the network weights and the ticker log levels $\ln\theta_i$ jointly by minimizing the mean squared difference between predicted and reported IV, which weights every retained observation equally. The loss was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nn_loss}
 \min_{w,\{\ln\theta_i\}}
 \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
 \left[
 \sqrt{\theta_{i(j)}\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau_j,m_j;w)}
 -\sigma_{\mathrm{market},j}
 \right]^2,
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of observations, $i(j)$ is the ticker of observation $j$, $\tau_j$ and $m_j$ are its calendar DTE and moneyness, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{market},j}$ is its reported IV. We initialized each log level at twice the logarithm of the ticker's mean reported IV. Training used the Adam optimizer with a decreasing learning rate for at most 2{,}000 epochs, or full passes through the training data. It stopped after 200 epochs without improvement in training loss, and we kept the weights from the final epoch. A constant can move between $\ln\theta_i$ and the network's output bias without changing the fit, so the two are not separately identified. Only the predicted variance $\theta_i\psi$ in~\eqref{eq:sigma_model} can be interpreted without an extra normalization.

To test how well the surfaces transferred to dates withheld from fitting, we used two holdouts within the calibration window. The April earnings holdout is named for the earnings reports near its test dates. It trained a network pooled over all tickers on the six snapshots labeled April 14 through April 22 and tested it on the snapshots labeled April 23 and 24. The single-date holdout withheld the snapshot of the May 11 session and retrained the sector networks on the other fourteen. To test whether scheduled earnings reports explained errors in this holdout, we also trained the pooled network on the same split with two earnings inputs, built from earnings dates in the public Yahoo Finance calendar. The first input was the signed number of calendar days to the ticker's nearest earnings date, positive before the report and negative after it, clipped to $[-30,30]$. The second was the smallest absolute number of days to an earnings date among the ticker's sector peers, clipped to 30. For the three ETFs, which report no earnings of their own, both inputs were the smallest absolute number of days to an earnings date among all 28 stocks. This network had 369 parameters ($4\to16\to16\to1$). The GS and LLY simulations never used the earnings inputs.

\subsection{Daily IV updates}\label{sec:theta_function}

The daily IV update adapted the variance equation of the Heston model~\cite{heston1993}. In its standard form, that model pairs a stock-price diffusion with a square-root process for the stock's instantaneous variance. The variance reverts toward a long-run level, and its random changes scale with the square root of its current value. It follows the stochastic differential equation given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heston_standard}
    dv_t = \kappa(\bar v-v_t)\,dt + \sigma_v\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^v ,
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the mean-reversion rate, $\bar v$ is the long-run variance, $\sigma_v$ is the volatility of variance, and $W_t^v$ is a Brownian motion that the standard model correlates with the one driving the stock. We applied the same square-root equation to a different quantity. Each option received its own implied-variance state, which we call its IV factor. The factor is an implied variance, and its square root is the option's IV. For an option $a=(i,K,T)$ on ticker $i$ with strike $K$ and expiration $T$, the factor $v_{a,t}$ reverts toward a moving target in place of Heston's constant level $\bar v$. The target is read from the fitted surface and is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta}
    \bar v_{a,t}=\theta_i\,
    \psi_{\mathrm{NN}}\!\left(\tau_t,K/S_t\right),
\end{equation}
where $\theta_i$ and $\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}$ are the fitted ticker level and neural shape for ticker $i$, $S_t$ is the stock price on date $t$, and $\tau_t$ is the remaining calendar DTE on that date. The target therefore moves as the stock price and the time to expiration change. The GS and LLY illustrations and the IV ablation used the per-ticker networks, and the forecast test used sector networks refitted on data through July 31. When the stock moved so far that $K/S_t$ left the fitted range of 0.8 to 1.2, the surface extrapolated, and we report how often this happened (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ablation_seeds}). Each factor started on the fitted surface on the entry date, April 28 in the illustrations:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:v0}
    v_{a,0}=\bar v_{a,0}.
\end{equation}
The forecast test instead started each factor at the option's observed IV on the date the forecast was issued. On every date, the IV entering the option's binomial tree was the square root of the factor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_path}
    \sigma_{a,t}=\sqrt{v_{a,t}}.
\end{equation}
Each pricing calculation held this IV constant, and the factor changed only between trading days. The factor is therefore the implied variance of one option, not the instantaneous variance of the stock's returns.

Each factor received a random shock on every trading day, partly tied to the stock's return. We first standardized the stock's daily log returns. The standardized return shock was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:return_shock}
    Z^S_{j,t}=\frac{\Delta\ln S_{j,t}-\overline{\Delta\ln S}}
    {s_{\Delta\ln S}},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\ln S_{j,t}=\ln(S_{j,t+1}/S_{j,t})$ is the log return on path $j$ over the step from date $t$ to date $t+1$, and $\overline{\Delta\ln S}$ and $s_{\Delta\ln S}$ are a mean and standard deviation of these returns. In the GS and LLY illustrations, these two constants came from all simulated paths and dates. The IV ablation and the forecast test instead took them, together with the growth shift, from a separate pilot simulation run before the evaluation paths were drawn. No evaluation path was therefore standardized with its own later returns. The shock to each factor combined the return shock with an independent standard normal shock and was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variance_shock}
    Z^v_{j,t}=\rho Z^S_{j,t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,Z^{\perp}_{j,t},
    \qquad Z^{\perp}_{j,t}\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ sets how strongly the factor responds to the stock's return. We fixed $\rho=-0.6$ by assumption rather than estimating it from market data, so IV tended to rise when the stock fell. On a given path and date, every option on the same stock received the same shock $Z^v_{j,t}$, so the put and the call in each illustration responded to the same random draws. Each option still kept its own factor and target, because the surface predicts a different IV at each strike and expiration. Each factor then advanced once per trading day by an Euler step of the square-root equation, with $\Delta t=1/252$ and a floor at $v_{\min}$. The step used $Z^v_{j,t}$ in place of a normal Brownian increment, so the part of each shock tied to the stock inherited the non-normal shape of the JumpHMM returns. The floored step was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler}
 v_{a,t+1}=\max\!\left\{
 v_{a,t}+\kappa(\bar v_{a,t}-v_{a,t})\Delta t
 +\sigma_v\sqrt{\max(v_{a,t},0)}\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z^v_{j,t},
 v_{\min}\right\},
\end{equation}
where the path index $j$ is omitted from $v_{a,t}$ and $\bar v_{a,t}$ for brevity. We fixed $\kappa=15$ per year, $\sigma_v=0.5$, and $v_{\min}=(0.005)^2$ as assumptions rather than estimates. With daily steps, $\kappa=15$ closes about 6\% of the gap between a factor and its target each trading day. The floor kept the factor positive for the pricer. It was needed because an Euler step can fall below zero even when the Feller condition, which keeps the continuous-time process positive, holds~\cite{andersen2008}. The simulation code also contains two optional features, a target that depends on JumpHMM's hidden state and a multiplier that raises the target during visits to tail states. Both were switched off in every analysis, so the fitted surface alone set the target and $\rho$ alone linked the random shocks to stock returns.

\subsection{American-option pricing and profit and loss}

A binomial tree prices an American option by working backward from expiration and taking, at every node, the larger of the value of continuing and the value of exercising immediately~\cite{cox1979,broadie1996}. We used two such trees, each with one constant volatility at every node. The Cox--Ross--Rubinstein (CRR) tree sets its up and down moves from that volatility~\cite{cox1979}. The Leisen--Reimer (LR) tree uses the Peizer--Pratt inversion to place its moves so that prices converge faster as steps are added, which gives the smooth prices that finite-difference Greeks need~\cite{leisen1996}. Moment-matching trees such as those of Jarrow--Rudd and Tian~\cite{jarrow1983,tian1993} are further alternatives that we did not use. To turn fitted IVs into dollar prices for comparison with the quoted midpoints in the first analysis, we used a 200-step American CRR tree. All simulated prices and Greeks used a 201-step American LR tree, since the LR construction requires an odd number of steps. When the option's IV $\sigma_{a,t}$ was so small that $\sigma_{a,t}\sqrt{\tau_t/365}<0.01$, with $\tau_t/365$ the time to expiration in years, or the LR tree gave no finite price, an American CRR tree with at least as many steps priced the option instead. Both trees priced options risk-neutrally with an assumed constant risk-free rate of $4.25\%$. This rate is separate from the zero rate that defines JumpHMM's growth rates, and the simulated stock paths followed JumpHMM's real-world growth rates rather than the risk-free rate. Both also set the dividend yield to zero ($q=0$) for every ticker, although GS, LLY, and many other tickers pay dividends. We accepted both mismatches as simplifications. With no dividends and a positive rate, early exercise of an American call is never optimal, so the American feature mattered only for the puts.

The GS and LLY illustrations each simulated one put and one call, opened on April 28, 2026, and expiring on May 29, 2026. On April 28, GS closed at \$926.38, and its options had strikes of \$890 (put, $K/S=0.961$) and \$970 (call, $K/S=1.047$). For LLY, the close was \$873.83, and the options had strikes of \$825 (put, $K/S=0.944$) and \$940 (call, $K/S=1.076$). All four options were therefore out of the money. We studied short positions, sold at the indicative midpoints in the April 28 snapshot, whose label, download, and session dates all agree, because a seller can lose money before expiration even when the option expires worthless. The holding period covered 31 calendar days and 22 trading days after excluding weekends and the May 25 market holiday. The tree measured time to expiration in calendar days and received $\tau_t/365$ years, while the stock and the factors advanced in 22 daily steps of $\Delta t=1/252$. The illustrations used 1{,}000 paths per ticker with seed 20260429. Each path started at the April 28 closing price, with its hidden state drawn from the stationary distribution of the transition matrix. Its 22 simulated returns then gave the prices on the 22 later trading days. Because the calibration window ran to May 11, the surfaces used in the illustrations and the IV ablation included quotes from after April 28, so these analyses illustrate the model rather than forecast. At expiration, the option's intrinsic value, its payoff from immediate exercise, replaced the tree price (Algorithm~\ref{alg:forward_sim}).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Forward simulation of contract values under the full factor; the other IV variants change only the factor update}\label{alg:forward_sim}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require JumpHMM model for ticker $i$ and growth shift $c$; fitted $(\theta_i,\psi_{\mathrm{NN}})$; contracts $a=(K,T,\phi)$, with $\phi=1$ for a call and $\phi=-1$ for a put; trading dates $d_0,\ldots,d_L$; calendar DTE $\tau_0,\ldots,\tau_L$; $(\kappa,\sigma_v,\rho)$; entry price $S_0$; risk-free rate $r$; $N_p$ paths; LR tree steps $N_{\mathrm{LR}}$.
\Ensure Stock, IV-factor, IV, and option-value paths.
\State Draw $N_p$ JumpHMM stock paths with $L$ trading transitions and emissions truncated as in~\eqref{eq:truncated_emission}.
\State Compute $Z^S_{j,\ell}$ by standardizing the simulated daily log returns as in~\eqref{eq:return_shock}, with constants from these paths or from a pilot simulation.
\State Draw $Z^\perp_{j,\ell}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ once per path and transition; set $Z^v_{j,\ell}\gets\rho Z^S_{j,\ell}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}Z^\perp_{j,\ell}$ and reuse it for every contract.
\For{contract $a$ and path $j$}
  \State $v_{a,0}^{(j)} \gets \theta_i\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau_0,K/S_0)$
  \For{$\ell=0$ \textbf{to} $L-1$}
    \State $\sigma_{a,\ell}^{(j)} \gets \sqrt{v_{a,\ell}^{(j)}}$
    \State $P_{a,\ell}^{(j)} \gets \mathrm{LR}_{N_{\mathrm{LR}}}(S_{j,\ell},K,\sigma_{a,\ell}^{(j)},\tau_\ell/365,r)$
    \State $\bar v_{a,\ell}^{(j)}\gets\theta_i\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau_\ell,K/S_{j,\ell})$
    \State Update $v_{a,\ell+1}^{(j)}$ with the floored Euler step in~\eqref{eq:euler} using $\Delta t=1/252$.
  \EndFor
  \State $P_{a,L}^{(j)}\gets\max\{\phi(S_{j,L}-K),0\}$.
\EndFor
\State \Return all paths.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For a short position sold at premium $p_0$, profit and loss (P\&L) at expiration was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:terminal_pnl}
 \Pi_T^{\mathrm{put}}=p_0^{\mathrm{put}}-\max(K-S_T,0),
 \qquad
 \Pi_T^{\mathrm{call}}=p_0^{\mathrm{call}}-\max(S_T-K,0),
\end{equation}
where $S_T$ is the JumpHMM stock price at expiration. We assumed entry at the observed midpoint, no transaction costs, no margin constraint, and no interest on the premium received. We also ignored early assignment. Each short position stayed open until expiration, or in the IV ablation until we closed it after five, ten, fifteen, or twenty trading days, even though the tree prices include the holder's right to exercise early. With early assignment ignored, the P\&L at expiration depends only on $S_T$, the strike, and the premium, so the IV updates changed option prices and Greeks before expiration but not the payoff of a position held to expiration. All premiums, option values, and P\&L are in dollars per share, so amounts for a standard 100-share contract are 100 times these values. We calculated the Greeks on the same LR tree by central finite differences, which reprice the option after equal upward and downward changes to one input. Delta measures the price's sensitivity to the stock price, gamma measures how delta changes with the stock price, and Vega measures the price's sensitivity to IV. Delta and gamma used stock-price bumps of $\pm1.5\%$. Vega used IV bumps of $\pm0.005$ in absolute terms and is reported per volatility percentage point.

\subsection{Comparison of five IV variants}\label{sec:ablation_methods}

To find which parts of the IV update changed option values before expiration, we repriced the GS and LLY options, sold at the same April 28 premiums, under five IV variants in the IV ablation. Within each seed, the five variants shared the same simulated stock paths. The first ablation seed equals the illustration seed, but the ablation set its growth shift from the pilot simulation, so its paths are not the illustration paths. Frozen IV kept each option's IV at its fitted entry value. The direct surface set the factor equal to its target, $v_{a,t}=\bar v_{a,t}$, on every date. Deterministic mean reversion used the update in~\eqref{eq:euler} without shocks ($\sigma_v=0$). The uncoupled stochastic factor added shocks unrelated to stock returns ($\sigma_v=0.5$, $\rho=0$). The full factor linked those shocks to stock returns ($\sigma_v=0.5$, $\rho=-0.6$). All three variants that followed~\eqref{eq:euler} used $\kappa=15$. Every variant started from the same fitted entry IV, and the two stochastic variants reused the same independent normal shocks, so results could be compared path by path. We ran the ablation with three random-number seeds per ticker (the integers 20260429, 20260430, and 20260431) and 1{,}000 paths per seed. Its pilot simulation used 10{,}000 paths (seed 20260905) and the same 22 trading-day steps. It fixed the growth shift for the 10\% target and the return mean and standard deviation before the evaluation paths were drawn.

We compared the variants by the P\&L from closing each short position before expiration. The primary endpoint closed the position after ten trading days, on May 12 with 17 calendar days to expiration. Secondary endpoints closed it after five, fifteen, and twenty trading days. We used the direct surface as the baseline, because its IV sits exactly on the target that the three updating variants move toward. The P\&L from closing early, and its difference from the direct surface on the same path, were given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ablation_pnl}
    \Pi_{a,t}^{(g,j)}=p_{a,0}-P_{a,t}^{(g,j)},
    \qquad D_{a,t}^{(g,j)}=\Pi_{a,t}^{(g,j)}-\Pi_{a,t}^{(\mathrm{surface},j)},
\end{equation}
where $g$ denotes the IV variant, $j$ the shared stock path, $p_{a,0}$ the entry premium, and $P_{a,t}^{(g,j)}$ the option's tree price on date $t$. Because the premium is the market midpoint while later prices come from the tree, $\Pi$ also includes the gap between the two at entry, and this gap cancels in $D$. For each variant, we reported the mean of $\Pi$, its standard deviation, its 5\% quantile, and its 5\% expected shortfall, defined as the mean P\&L at or below that quantile. The Monte Carlo standard error of the mean paired difference was $s_D/\sqrt{N_p}$, where $s_D$ is the standard deviation of $D$ across the $N_p$ paths, 1{,}000 per seed or 3{,}000 pooled. These standard errors measure simulation noise with the fitted models and pilot constants held fixed. Price differences on individual paths can cancel in the mean, so we also reported the mean absolute difference between each variant's price and the direct-surface price on the same path. We reported each seed separately and also pooled the 3{,}000 paths per ticker.

We checked that entry prices and payoffs at expiration agreed exactly across variants, as they must by construction. We also checked the numerical accuracy of the prices in between. For every variant, we compared 201-step and 401-step LR prices on the first twenty paths of every seed after ten and twenty trading days and recorded the largest absolute difference. The tree prices each option separately, so nothing forces prices to be consistent across strikes. We therefore priced calls and puts at eleven fixed strikes from 0.8 to 1.2 times the entry stock price, a check we call the strike audit. The audit ran within the first ablation seed. Each audit strike used the May 29 expiration and its own factor and target, driven by the same shocks as the GS or LLY options. The audit used the first fifty paths of the first seed at entry and after ten and twenty trading days, at both 201 and 401 steps. Entry, which all paths share, was checked once. Each ticker, variant, option type, and tree size therefore had 101 stock-price points, the entry price and fifty paths on each of two dates. At each of these points, we checked four conditions, and a violation's size was the dollar amount by which a condition failed. Each price had to lie between its intrinsic value and an upper bound, the stock price for a call and the strike for a put. As the strike increased, call prices could not rise and put prices could not fall. The price difference between adjacent strikes could not exceed the distance between the strikes (the vertical-spread bound). Prices had to be convex in strike, so no price could exceed the straight line between its neighbors. For all four checks, we counted violations larger than \$0.01 per share. Passing these checks is necessary, but not sufficient, for prices to be free of arbitrage across strikes. The audit compared strikes within one expiration on each date, so it did not test consistency across expiration dates.
