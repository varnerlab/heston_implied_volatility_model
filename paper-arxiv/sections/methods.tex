\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Pipeline and stock-path generation}

The framework connected stock-path generation to contract-specific IV dynamics and American-option valuation, with information passing through three stages in this order:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pipeline}
    \text{JumpHMM stock path}
    \longrightarrow \text{contract IV factor}
    \longrightarrow \text{American lattice price and Greeks}.
\end{equation}
Alpaca option data entered during the offline surface fit, after which each simulated step used that fitted surface without requiring a new option quote or feeding lattice prices back into the fit. Stock paths came from pretrained single-ticker JumpHMM marginals fitted to equity histories from 2014--2024~\cite{jumphmm2025}. The model represents excess growth rates with latent states and location-scale Student-$t$ emissions, and a jump mechanism increases visits to tail states to reproduce features such as heavy tails and volatility clustering~\cite{cont2001}. The pretrained artifact contained 424 ticker marginals with 50 states and 2{,}767 training days, and its growth rates used a $1/252$ year step and zero risk-free offset. A Student-$t$ copula can couple these marginals across assets~\cite{demarta2005}, although the reported scenarios used one ticker at a time.

For price simulation, we replaced the unbounded Student-$t$ emissions with their conditional distributions on a finite interval, so that the simulated excess growth rate in state $k$ was defined by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:truncated_emission}
 G_t=\mu_k+\sigma_k Z_t,\qquad
 Z_t\sim t_{\nu_k}\mid |Z_t|\le b,\qquad b=10.
\end{equation}
The locations, scales, transition matrix, and jump parameters retained their training-fitted values, because the cutoff was a simulation assumption applied after fitting and we did not refit the model under a truncated likelihood. The cutoff was measured in units of the fitted Student-$t$ scale rather than its standard deviation. We rejected and redrew out-of-bound emissions before applying the growth shift and converting log returns to prices, so every stock path was retained. This construction preserved the state and jump mechanism while giving finite stock-price moments at each fixed horizon, whereas exponentiating untruncated Student-$t$ log returns gives an infinite stock-price mean and invalidates finite population interpretations of mean call values and short-call expected losses~\cite{cassidy2009}. To examine sensitivity, we specified a wider cutoff $b=20$ with the same contracts, seeds, and evaluation dates, recomputed normalization constants for each cutoff, and selected neither cutoff by forecast performance (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:emission_bounds}, \ref{tab:tail_sensitivity}, and~\ref{tab:tail_forecasts}).

\subsection{Offline IV-surface calibration}

We downloaded option-chain snapshots daily from Alpaca Markets using a free account, which supplied Alpaca's indicative feed, whose quotes are modified derivatives of the exchange feed rather than actual OPRA quotes~\cite{alpaca_option_feed}. All option quote, midpoint, spread, and reported-IV comparisons in this study therefore refer to these captured vendor values and do not measure agreement with exchange-observed or executable prices. The frozen corpus contained 457 ticker files in fifteen snapshot directories and 234{,}549 observations after filtering, which required calls and puts to have a positive bid, positive calendar days to expiration (DTE), reported IV strictly between 0.01 and 2.0, and strike/spot between 0.8 and 1.2 inclusive, with spot taken as the stored underlying-session close. Both option types entered the same loss, and repeated contracts across snapshots remained separate observations. The calibration target was Alpaca's reported IV field, which Alpaca documents as a Black--Scholes calculation with an iterative solver based on price sensitivity to volatility (Vega)~\cite{alpaca_market_data_faq}. Snapshot labels and underlying-session dates defined the respective temporal splits (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:corpus_dates}). This corpus provided extensive cross-sectional coverage over a short observation window, which we used to compare a five-parameter log-polynomial with neural surfaces shared globally, within sectors, or fitted separately to individual tickers. Parametric models such as SABR and SVI motivate compact representations of IV smiles~\cite{hagan2002,gatheral2004}, whereas neural representations allow more flexible shapes~\cite{horvath2021,bayer2019}. We exponentiated each shape function to keep its output positive but imposed neither calendar monotonicity nor strike convexity during fitting, so the fitted surfaces required separate no-arbitrage checks. For ticker $i$, calendar days to expiration $\tau$, and moneyness $m=K/S$, we used the following parametric shape:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi}
 \psi_{\beta}(\tau,m)=\exp\!\left[
 \beta_1\ln\tau+\beta_2\ln m+\beta_3\ln\tau\ln m
 +\beta_4(\ln m)^2+\beta_5(\ln\tau)^2\right].
\end{equation}
The corresponding IV prediction combined a ticker level with the surface shape:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_model}
    \widehat\sigma_i^2(\tau,m)=\theta_i\,\psi(\tau,m),
    \qquad \theta_i>0,\quad \psi(\tau,m)>0,
\end{equation}
and the fitted squared IV also defined the variance target on each observation date:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta_calibration}
    \theta_{\mathrm{cal}}(i,\tau,m)=\theta_i\,\psi(\tau,m).
\end{equation}

The neural network (NN) models replaced the log-polynomial with a positive network output, which defined the two-input neural surface:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi_nn}
    \psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau,m;w)
    =\exp\!\left[\mathrm{NN}\!\left(
    \operatorname{std}(\ln\tau),\operatorname{std}(\ln m);w\right)\right].
\end{equation}
Inputs were standardized using the training rows. Per-ticker fits required at least 2{,}000 observations, and tickers below that threshold retained sector fits. Networks with at least 5{,}000 per-ticker observations, or 2{,}000 pooled group observations, used a $2\to16\to16\to1$ architecture with $\tanh$ activations and 337 parameters, while smaller qualified groups used $2\to8\to8\to1$ with 105 parameters. For the earnings analysis, we appended two standardized inputs, the signed days to the ticker's nearest earnings date clipped to $[-30,30]$ and the minimum absolute earnings distance among same-sector peers clipped to 30, which gave a $4\to16\to16\to1$ network with 369 parameters. Earnings dates came from the public Yahoo Finance calendar, and these event features entered only the temporal calibration comparison and remained inactive in the GS and LLY scenario drivers. We fitted each network representation by optimizing its weights and ticker log levels jointly to minimize the average squared difference between predicted and reported IV, a criterion that assigned equal weight to every retained observation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nn_loss}
 \min_{w,\{\ln\theta_i\}}
 \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
 \left[
 \sqrt{\theta_{i(j)}\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau_j,m_j;w)}
 -\sigma_{\mathrm{market},j}
 \right]^2.
\end{equation}
The log levels were initialized from each ticker's mean squared market IV, and Adam training used a decreasing learning-rate schedule, at most 2{,}000 epochs, and a best-training-loss checkpoint with patience 200. Because a constant can be shifted between $\ln\theta_i$ and the output bias of the network, these two components are not separately identified, and only their product in~\eqref{eq:sigma_model} is interpretable without an additional normalization constraint.

\subsection{Contract-specific dynamic IV factors}\label{sec:theta_function}

The standard Heston model couples an asset-price diffusion to a square-root instantaneous-variance process~\cite{heston1993}, whose variance follows the stochastic differential equation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heston_standard}
    dv_t = \kappa(\theta-v_t)\,dt + \sigma_v\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^v .
\end{equation}
Here $\kappa$ is the mean-reversion rate, $\theta$ is long-run variance, and $\sigma_v$ is volatility of variance, and the standard model correlates the variance Brownian motion with the Brownian motion driving the stock. We instead used the square-root process to evolve a separate implied-variance state for each contract along a stock path supplied by JumpHMM. Each state reverted toward a target from the fitted surface and supplied the volatility input for that contract's lattice price, which the lattice treated as constant within each pricing calculation even though it changed between simulated dates. The square-root process therefore served as a dynamic IV factor rather than as the underlying asset's instantaneous return variance in a standard Heston economy. Within this construction, each scenario contract $a=(i,K,T)$ received a variance state $v_{a,t}$ whose target changed with spot and remaining calendar DTE, and we obtained that target by evaluating the fitted surface at the current contract state:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta}
    \bar v_{a,t}=\theta_i\,
    \psi_{\mathrm{NN}}\!\left(\tau_t,K/S_t\right).
\end{equation}
The factor started exactly on the fitted entry surface,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:v0}
    v_{a,0}=\bar v_{a,0},
\end{equation}
and at each later date the lattice IV was the square root of that contract's factor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_path}
    \sigma_{a,t}=\sqrt{v_{a,t}}.
\end{equation}

The put and call on a stock shared its path and variance shock but retained their own variance states and surface targets, and the stock and factor updates advanced only on exchange sessions. For the original fitted illustrations, we shifted every simulated growth-rate observation by a constant so that the ensemble mean matched a prespecified annual growth prior and then standardized the one-session log returns across all paths and dates, whereas the paired ablation used independent pilot estimates for these constants. The standardized return shock entering the IV update was defined by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:return_shock}
    Z^S_{j,t}=\frac{\Delta\ln S_{j,t}-\overline{\Delta\ln S}}
    {s_{\Delta\ln S}}.
\end{equation}
An independent standard normal shock supplied the remaining variation, and combining the return and independent shocks gave the factor shock:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variance_shock}
    Z^v_{j,t}=\rho Z^S_{j,t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,Z^{\perp}_{j,t},
    \qquad Z^{\perp}_{j,t}\sim\mathcal N(0,1).
\end{equation}
The coefficient $\rho$ was fixed at $-0.6$ and acted as a return-shock coupling parameter rather than a calibrated correlation between two Brownian motions in a standard Heston model. We advanced each factor with a hard-floor Euler update and trading-year step $\Delta t=1/252$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler}
 v_{a,t+1}=\max\!\left\{
 v_{a,t}+\kappa(\bar v_{a,t}-v_{a,t})\Delta t
 +\sigma_v\sqrt{\max(v_{a,t},0)}\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z^v_{j,t},
 v_{\min}\right\}.
\end{equation}
We fixed $\kappa=15$, $\sigma_v=0.5$, and $v_{\min}=(0.005)^2$ as scenario assumptions, and the floor protected the numerical pricer when these parameters did not satisfy the Feller condition~\cite{andersen2008}. The repository also contains a proposed state-dependent level and aggregate tail-state mood multiplier, but both terms remained inactive in every reported experiment, so the fitted surface determined the target throughout the study and the fixed coefficient $\rho$ determined return-shock coupling. Evaluating the additional mechanisms would require state-conditioned option histories.

\subsection{American-option pricing, calendar clock, and terminal profit and loss}

Recombining lattices price American options by comparing continuation with immediate exercise at every node during backward induction~\cite{broadie1996}. The Cox--Ross--Rubinstein (CRR) construction sets its up and down factors from the supplied volatility~\cite{cox1979}, and alternatives include the moment matches of Jarrow--Rudd and Tian~\cite{jarrow1983,tian1993} and the Peizer--Pratt inversion used by Leisen--Reimer to improve finite-step convergence~\cite{leisen1996}. Both pricers used here accepted one IV value for a given contract and date. The GS and LLY contracts ran from April 28 to May 29, 2026, an interval of 31 calendar days and 22 exchange-session transitions after excluding weekends and the May 25 market holiday. The surface and lattice used calendar DTE, with $\tau_t/365$ years entering the pricer, while the stock and variance states advanced on the 22 trading transitions. Within this common clock, corpus-wide dollar-price checks used a 200-step CRR American tree and scenario marks and Greeks used a 201-step Leisen--Reimer (LR) American tree, both with a constant risk-free rate of $4.25\%$ and continuous dividend yield $q=0$. For low-volatility states or saturated Leisen--Reimer probabilities, an American CRR tree supplied the mark and retained the immediate-exercise comparison. At each trading transition, the simulation priced each contract at its current stock price, remaining maturity, and factor value before advancing the factor toward its moving target, and at expiration it replaced the lattice value with intrinsic value (Supplementary Algorithm~\ref{alg:forward_sim}).

We calculated terminal profit and loss for a short position that received the captured indicative midpoint $p_0$ as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:terminal_pnl}
 \Pi_T^{\mathrm{put}}=p_0^{\mathrm{put}}-\max(K-S_T,0),
 \qquad
 \Pi_T^{\mathrm{call}}=p_0^{\mathrm{call}}-\max(S_T-K,0).
\end{equation}
Terminal profit and loss in~\eqref{eq:terminal_pnl} therefore depended only on the JumpHMM terminal stock price, strike, and premium, so dynamic IV affected interim option marks and Greeks but not a held-to-expiration payoff. All option premiums, values, and P\&L are dollars per underlying share, so monetary amounts for a standard 100-share contract are 100 times these values, and premium financing was omitted. We calculated price sensitivities, or Greeks, by central finite differences on the same Leisen--Reimer pricer, with delta measuring price sensitivity to spot and gamma measuring the change in delta with spot. Spot bumps were $\pm1.5\%$ of spot for delta and gamma, and volatility bumps were $\pm0.005$, with Vega reported per one volatility percentage point. We assumed execution at the observed mid, no transaction costs, no margin constraint, no early assignment before the plotted horizon, and no discrete dividends.

\subsection{Paired dynamic IV ablation}\label{sec:ablation_methods}

We tested which IV components changed interim valuation by holding the stock paths, contracts, entry premiums, and pricing assumptions fixed across five variants. Frozen IV retained each contract's fitted entry value of $v$, the direct surface set $v_{a,t}=\bar v_{a,t}$ at every date, and deterministic mean reversion used~\eqref{eq:euler} with $\sigma_v=0$. An uncoupled stochastic factor used $\sigma_v=0.5$ and $\rho=0$, and the full factor used $\sigma_v=0.5$ and $\rho=-0.6$, with $\kappa=15$ in all three evolving variants. Every variant started at the same fitted entry IV and reused the same independent normal innovation wherever a stochastic factor was active. The evaluation applied this paired design to three seeds per ticker, 20260429, 20260430, and 20260431, with 1{,}000 paths per seed. A separate 10{,}000-path pilot with seed 20260905 supplied a constant growth shift to the 10\% annual prior and the resulting one-session log-return mean and standard deviation, and we froze those constants before generating evaluation paths, so that, unlike the original fitted illustrations, the evaluation paths were not recentered or standardized using their own future realizations. The pilot and evaluation simulations each used 22 transitions, and every return was applied once after the initial stock price.

The primary endpoint was liquidation after ten trading transitions, on May 12 with 17 calendar days remaining, and secondary endpoints used five, fifteen, and twenty transitions. The short-position liquidation P\&L and its paired difference from the direct-surface reference were given by the following expressions:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ablation_pnl}
    \Pi_{a,t}^{(b,j)}=p_{a,0}-P_{a,t}^{(b,j)},
    \qquad D_{a,t}^{(b,j)}=\Pi_{a,t}^{(b,j)}-\Pi_{a,t}^{(\mathrm{surface},j)}.
\end{equation}
Here $b$ denotes the IV variant and $j$ the shared stock path. We reported mean P\&L, its standard deviation, the 5\% quantile, and 5\% expected shortfall, defined as mean P\&L at or below that quantile. The paired mean difference had Monte Carlo standard error $s_D/\sqrt{n}$, with smaller values indicating more precise simulation estimates of that mean, and mean absolute mark differences measured the size of pathwise changes even when positive and negative differences canceled in the mean. We reported each seed separately and pooled 3{,}000 paths per ticker, and these uncertainty estimates were conditional on the fitted models and pilot constants.

We also checked exact agreement of entry marks and terminal payoffs across variants and examined the numerical accuracy and strike consistency of the intervening prices. Numerical checks compared 201- and 401-step marks on the first twenty paths of every evaluation seed at transitions ten and twenty. A separate strike audit used eleven fixed strikes from 0.8 to 1.2 times entry spot, the first fifty paths of the first seed, and transitions zero, ten, and twenty, with calls and puts at the scenario expiry sharing the same shocks. At both tree depths, we checked intrinsic and upper price bounds, strike monotonicity, vertical-spread bounds, and convexity, measuring convexity violations as the dollar excess above the neighboring linear interpolation and counting violations larger than \$0.01 per share, with entry counted once per variant and option type. These conditions are necessary for strike consistency, and the audit did not test calendar or dynamic no-arbitrage.
