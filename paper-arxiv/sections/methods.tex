\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Simulator overview and stock paths}

The simulator valued options along stock paths in two phases. We first fitted IV surfaces to observed quotes, predicting IV from moneyness (strike divided by stock price) and time to expiration. We then generated complete stock paths and advanced along each path one trading day at a time. A binomial tree used the current stock price, remaining time, and each option's IV to calculate its price and price sensitivities (Greeks). For the next trading day, we updated each option's implied variance, the square of IV. The update combined a pull toward a variance target from the fitted surface with random shocks partly tied to the stock return over that step. We evaluated the target before advancing the stock price and time to expiration. Starting IVs came from the fitted surfaces, except in the forecast test, which used observed IVs. No subsequent quotes entered a simulation. Option prices never fed back into the surfaces or stock paths (Algorithm~\ref{alg:forward_sim}). Illustrations used short put and call positions on Goldman Sachs (GS) and Eli Lilly (LLY). To isolate the effect of IV, we repriced the same options on identical stock paths under five IV rules, a comparison we call the IV ablation. Separate tests assessed forecasts of option and stock prices.

The stock paths came from the Jump Hidden Markov Model (JumpHMM)~\cite{jumphmm2025}. We used models from the study that introduced JumpHMM, which fitted 424 tickers separately on 2{,}767 trading days from 2014 to 2024. The original fit assigned historical excess growth rates, the annualized log returns minus a risk-free rate, to 50 ordered bins and used bin membership as the state labels for fitting. Each state drew this rate from its own location-scale Student-$t$ distribution, the state's emission distribution. The models used a time step of $1/252$ year and a risk-free rate of zero, so their growth rates were annualized log returns. During jump episodes, selection of states from the lowest or highest bins replaced ordinary state transitions for a Poisson-distributed number of days. The original study tuned the frequency and duration of these episodes. Every simulation here used one ticker at a time.

For price simulation, we bounded the Student-$t$ emissions because exponentiating unbounded Student-$t$ log returns gives an infinite expected stock price, leaving mean call values and expected short-call losses without finite population values~\cite{cassidy2009}. The simulated growth rate on day $t$ was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:truncated_emission}
 G_t=\mu_{k_t}+\sigma_{k_t} Z_t,\qquad
 Z_t\sim t_{\nu_{k_t}}\mid |Z_t|\le b,\qquad b=10,
\end{equation}
where $k_t$ is the hidden state on day $t$, $\mu_k$, $\sigma_k$, and $\nu_k$ are its fitted location, scale, and degrees of freedom, and $Z_t$ is a Student-$t$ draw restricted to $|Z_t|\le b$. The cutoff $b$ is measured in multiples of the state's scale. We replaced draws outside the bound with draws from the restricted distribution; no stock path was discarded. This change applied only during simulation, preserved the fitted parameters and state transitions, and gave stock prices finite means and variances at every fixed horizon. Prices followed $S_{t+1}=S_t\exp[(G_t+c)\Delta t]$ with $\Delta t=1/252$. To replace the drift inherited from 2014--2024, we estimated the constant growth shift $c$ separately for each ticker. The shifted annualized log-growth rates $G_t+c$ averaged an assumed 10\% in the estimation sample. The illustrations estimated $c$ from their sampled growth rates before converting them to prices. The IV ablation and forecast test used a separate pilot simulation, then drew fresh paths for scoring, called evaluation paths, which never influenced $c$. We chose $b=10$ and a wider $b=20$ sensitivity case in advance as stress assumptions, not by forecast performance. The wider-cutoff reruns repeated the stock and option simulations with the same options, seeds, and dates. They recomputed $c$ and the mean and standard deviation used to center and scale stock log returns for the stock-related IV shocks (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:emission_bounds}, \ref{tab:tail_sensitivity}, and~\ref{tab:tail_forecasts}).

\subsection{Option data and IV surfaces}

We fitted surfaces to the IVs reported in Alpaca Markets' indicative option feed, available with a free account. The feed derives from consolidated Options Price Reporting Authority (OPRA) data, but its quotes, midpoints, spreads, and IVs may differ from exchange quotes and executable prices~\cite{alpaca_option_feed}. Alpaca computes IV with the European Black--Scholes formula~\cite{alpaca_market_data_faq}, so using it in an American tree need not reproduce the quoted price. Each daily download, or snapshot, stored calls, puts, and a closing stock price in one file per ticker. We retained expirations nearest 2, 7, 14, 30, and 45 calendar days, with 60 and 90 days added on some dates. The calibration set contained fifteen snapshots labeled April 14 through May 11, 2026, with gaps between dates. Its 457 files covered 23 tickers initially and 31 thereafter: 28 stocks and three exchange-traded funds (ETFs). Folder labels, download dates, and closing-price session dates did not always agree (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:corpus_dates}). The April 14 snapshot stored the April 13 close, and the April 29 folder stored the April 30 close; labels and sessions agreed for April 28 and May 11. We retained 234{,}549 observations with positive bids, positive calendar days to expiration (DTE), reported IV strictly between 0.01 and 2.0, and moneyness $K/S$ between 0.8 and 1.2 inclusive, where $K$ is strike and $S$ is the stored stock price. Fits used the stored DTE, which counted from the download date rather than the session date in the April 23 and May 1 snapshots. Calls and puts shared the fitting loss; repeated options contributed once per snapshot. Downloads continued through September 4, 2026, supporting sector-network refits through July 31 and scoring on later dates.

We compared a parametric surface shared by all tickers with neural surfaces shared globally, by sector, or fitted separately to individual tickers. The six sectors comprised five industry groups and one group for the three ETFs (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:sector_map}). Every surface predicted squared IV as a ticker-specific level times a positive shape function of maturity and moneyness. Following the use of compact parametric surfaces such as SABR and SVI~\cite{hagan2002,gatheral2004}, we used a shared exponential-polynomial shape given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi}
 \psi_{\beta}(\tau,m)=\exp\!\left[
 \beta_1\ln\tau+\beta_2\ln m+\beta_3\ln\tau\ln m
 +\beta_4(\ln m)^2+\beta_5(\ln\tau)^2\right],
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is calendar DTE in days, $m=K/S$, and $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_5$ are fitted coefficients. For both parametric and neural shapes, the predicted squared IV for ticker $i$ was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_model}
    \widehat\sigma_i^2(\tau,m)=\theta_i\,\psi(\tau,m),
    \qquad \theta_i>0,\quad \psi(\tau,m)>0,
\end{equation}
where $\theta_i$ is the ticker's variance level and $\psi$ is the shape. The parametric fit jointly estimated the shared coefficients and ticker levels using the IV loss defined below. Fits did not require total implied variance, squared IV times time to expiration, to be nondecreasing across maturities at fixed moneyness, or option prices to be convex across strikes.

The neural shape replaced the polynomial with a neural network (NN) output and was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi_nn}
    \psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau,m;w)
    =\exp\!\left[\mathrm{NN}\!\left(
    \operatorname{std}(\ln\tau),\operatorname{std}(\ln m);w\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $w$ denotes network weights and $\operatorname{std}(\cdot)$ centers and scales each input using its training mean and standard deviation. Global and sector networks had two hidden layers of 16 $\tanh$ units ($2\to16\to16\to1$, 337 parameters). Individual tickers required at least 2{,}000 observations for their own network; those with fewer retained their sector network. Tickers with at least 5{,}000 observations used the same architecture as the global network; eligible tickers below that threshold used two layers of 8 units ($2\to8\to8\to1$, 105 parameters). We jointly fitted network weights and ticker log levels $\ln\theta_i$ by minimizing the equally weighted mean squared IV error, given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nn_loss}
 \min_{w,\{\ln\theta_i\}}
 \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
 \left[
 \sqrt{\theta_{i(j)}\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau_j,m_j;w)}
 -\sigma_{\mathrm{market},j}
 \right]^2,
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the observation count, $i(j)$ identifies the ticker, and $\tau_j$, $m_j$, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{market},j}$ are observation $j$'s DTE, moneyness, and reported IV. Each log level started at twice the logarithm of its ticker's mean IV. Training used Adam with a decreasing learning rate for at most 2{,}000 epochs, or full passes through the data, and stopped after 200 epochs without training-loss improvement. We retained the final weights. A constant can shift between $\ln\theta_i$ and the network's output bias without changing predictions, so only their combined variance prediction $\theta_i\psi$ in~\eqref{eq:sigma_model} is identifiable without further normalization.

We tested transfer to withheld dates using two holdouts within the calibration window. The single-date test fitted sector networks on fourteen snapshots and tested on May 11. The April earnings test fitted them on the six snapshots labeled April 14 through April 22 and tested on April 23 and 24. For the April test, three configurations assessed the role of nearby earnings reports, with all earnings distances measured from the snapshot's folder date. Configuration A used all rows and the two inputs, moneyness and maturity. Configuration B used those inputs but excluded training and test rows within three calendar days of the ticker's or a sector peer's earnings report, testing transfer away from earnings. Configuration C used all rows and added two inputs from the public Yahoo Finance earnings calendar. The first counted calendar days to the ticker's nearest report, positive before and negative after, clipped to $[-30,30]$. The second was the smallest absolute day distance to any sector peer's report, capped at 30. For the three ETFs, both inputs used the nearest report among all 28 stocks; that distance also determined their exclusions in B. The four-input networks had 369 parameters ($4\to16\to16\to1$). The GS and LLY simulations used no earnings inputs.

\subsection{Daily IV updates}\label{sec:theta_function}

The daily IV update adapted the variance equation of the Heston model~\cite{heston1993}. In its standard form, that model pairs a stock-price diffusion with a square-root process for the stock's instantaneous variance. The variance reverts toward a long-run level, and its random changes scale with the square root of its current value. It follows the stochastic differential equation given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heston_standard}
    dv_t = \kappa(\bar v-v_t)\,dt + \sigma_v\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^v ,
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the mean-reversion rate, $\bar v$ is the long-run variance, $\sigma_v$ is the volatility of variance, and $W_t^v$ is a Brownian motion that the standard model correlates with the one driving the stock. We applied the same square-root equation to a different quantity. Each option received its own implied-variance state, which we call its IV factor. The factor is an implied variance, and its square root is the option's IV. For an option $a=(i,K,T)$ on ticker $i$ with strike $K$ and expiration $T$, the factor $v_{a,t}$ reverts toward a moving target in place of Heston's constant level $\bar v$. The target is read from the fitted surface and is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta}
    \bar v_{a,t}=\theta_i\,
    \psi_{\mathrm{NN}}\!\left(\tau_t,K/S_t\right),
\end{equation}
where $\theta_i$ and $\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}$ are the fitted ticker level and neural shape for ticker $i$, $S_t$ is the stock price on date $t$, and $\tau_t$ is the remaining calendar DTE on that date. The target therefore moves as the stock price and the time to expiration change. The GS and LLY illustrations and the IV ablation used the per-ticker networks, and the forecast test used sector networks refitted on data through July 31. When the stock moved so far that $K/S_t$ left the fitted range of 0.8 to 1.2, the surface extrapolated, and we report how often this happened (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:ablation_seeds}). Each factor started on the fitted surface on the entry date, April 28 in the illustrations:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:v0}
    v_{a,0}=\bar v_{a,0}.
\end{equation}
The forecast test instead started each factor at the option's observed IV on the date the forecast was issued. On every date, the IV entering the option's binomial tree was the square root of the factor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_path}
    \sigma_{a,t}=\sqrt{v_{a,t}}.
\end{equation}
Each pricing calculation held this IV constant, and the factor changed only between trading days. The factor is therefore the implied variance of one option, not the instantaneous variance of the stock's returns.

Each factor received a random shock on every trading day, partly tied to the stock's return. We first standardized the stock's daily log returns. The standardized return shock was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:return_shock}
    Z^S_{j,t}=\frac{\Delta\ln S_{j,t}-\overline{\Delta\ln S}}
    {s_{\Delta\ln S}},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\ln S_{j,t}=\ln(S_{j,t+1}/S_{j,t})$ is the log return on path $j$ over the step from date $t$ to date $t+1$, and $\overline{\Delta\ln S}$ and $s_{\Delta\ln S}$ are a mean and standard deviation of these returns. In the GS and LLY illustrations, these two constants came from all simulated paths and dates. The IV ablation and the forecast test instead took them, together with the growth shift, from a separate pilot simulation run before the evaluation paths were drawn. No evaluation path was therefore standardized with its own later returns. The shock to each factor combined the return shock with an independent standard normal shock and was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variance_shock}
    Z^v_{j,t}=\rho Z^S_{j,t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,Z^{\perp}_{j,t},
    \qquad Z^{\perp}_{j,t}\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ sets how strongly the factor responds to the stock's return. We fixed $\rho=-0.6$ by assumption rather than estimating it from market data, so IV tended to rise when the stock fell. On a given path and date, every option on the same stock received the same shock $Z^v_{j,t}$, so the put and the call in each illustration responded to the same random draws. Each option still kept its own factor and target, because the surface predicts a different IV at each strike and expiration. Each factor then advanced once per trading day by an Euler step of the square-root equation, with $\Delta t=1/252$ and a floor at $v_{\min}$. The step used $Z^v_{j,t}$ in place of a normal Brownian increment, so the part of each shock tied to the stock inherited the non-normal shape of the JumpHMM returns. The floored step was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler}
 v_{a,t+1}=\max\!\left\{
 v_{a,t}+\kappa(\bar v_{a,t}-v_{a,t})\Delta t
 +\sigma_v\sqrt{\max(v_{a,t},0)}\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z^v_{j,t},
 v_{\min}\right\},
\end{equation}
where the path index $j$ is omitted from $v_{a,t}$ and $\bar v_{a,t}$ for brevity. We fixed $\kappa=15$ per year, $\sigma_v=0.5$, and $v_{\min}=(0.005)^2$ as assumptions rather than estimates. With daily steps, $\kappa=15$ closes about 6\% of the gap between a factor and its target each trading day. The floor kept the factor positive for the pricer. It was needed because an Euler step can fall below zero even when the Feller condition, which keeps the continuous-time process positive, holds~\cite{andersen2008}. The simulation code also contains two optional features, a target that depends on JumpHMM's hidden state and a multiplier that raises the target during visits to tail states. Both were switched off in every analysis, so the fitted surface alone set the target and $\rho$ alone linked the random shocks to stock returns.

\subsection{American-option pricing and profit and loss}

A binomial tree prices an American option by working backward from expiration and taking, at every node, the larger of the value of continuing and the value of exercising immediately~\cite{cox1979,broadie1996}. We used two such trees, each with one constant volatility at every node. The Cox--Ross--Rubinstein (CRR) tree sets its up and down moves from that volatility~\cite{cox1979}. The Leisen--Reimer (LR) tree uses the Peizer--Pratt inversion to place its moves so that prices converge faster as steps are added, which gives the smooth prices that finite-difference Greeks need~\cite{leisen1996}. Moment-matching trees such as those of Jarrow--Rudd and Tian~\cite{jarrow1983,tian1993} are further alternatives that we did not use. To turn fitted IVs into dollar prices for comparison with the quoted midpoints in the first analysis, we used a 200-step American CRR tree. All simulated prices and Greeks used a 201-step American LR tree, since the LR construction requires an odd number of steps. When the option's IV $\sigma_{a,t}$ was so small that $\sigma_{a,t}\sqrt{\tau_t/365}<0.01$, with $\tau_t/365$ the time to expiration in years, or the LR tree gave no finite price, an American CRR tree with at least as many steps priced the option instead. Both trees priced options risk-neutrally with an assumed constant risk-free rate of $4.25\%$. This rate is separate from the zero rate that defines JumpHMM's growth rates, and the simulated stock paths followed JumpHMM's real-world growth rates rather than the risk-free rate. Both also set the dividend yield to zero ($q=0$) for every ticker, although GS, LLY, and many other tickers pay dividends. We accepted both mismatches as simplifications. With no dividends and a positive rate, early exercise of an American call is never optimal, so the American feature mattered only for the puts.

The GS and LLY illustrations each simulated one put and one call, opened on April 28, 2026, and expiring on May 29, 2026. On April 28, GS closed at \$926.38, and its options had strikes of \$890 (put, $K/S=0.961$) and \$970 (call, $K/S=1.047$). For LLY, the close was \$873.83, and the options had strikes of \$825 (put, $K/S=0.944$) and \$940 (call, $K/S=1.076$). All four options were therefore out of the money. We modeled short positions entered at the indicative midpoints in the April 28 snapshot, whose label, download, and session dates all agree. The modeled cost of buying back an option can exceed its entry premium even if it later expires worthless. The holding period covered 31 calendar days and 22 trading days after excluding weekends and the May 25 market holiday. The tree measured time to expiration in calendar days and received $\tau_t/365$ years, while the stock and the factors advanced in 22 daily steps of $\Delta t=1/252$. The illustrations used 1{,}000 paths per ticker with seed 20260429. Each path started at the April 28 closing price, with its hidden state drawn from the stationary distribution of the transition matrix. Its 22 simulated returns then gave the prices on the 22 later trading days. Because the calibration window ran to May 11, the surfaces used in the illustrations and the IV ablation included quotes from after April 28, so these analyses illustrate the model rather than forecast. At expiration, the option's intrinsic value, its payoff from immediate exercise, replaced the tree price (Algorithm~\ref{alg:forward_sim}).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Forward simulation of contract values under the full factor; the other IV variants change only the factor update}\label{alg:forward_sim}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require JumpHMM model for ticker $i$ and growth shift $c$; fitted $(\theta_i,\psi_{\mathrm{NN}})$; contracts $a=(K,T,\phi)$, with $\phi=1$ for a call and $\phi=-1$ for a put; trading dates $d_0,\ldots,d_L$; calendar DTE $\tau_0,\ldots,\tau_L$; $(\kappa,\sigma_v,\rho)$; entry price $S_0$; risk-free rate $r$; $N_p$ paths; LR tree steps $N_{\mathrm{LR}}$.
\Ensure Stock, IV-factor, IV, and option-value paths.
\State Draw $N_p$ JumpHMM stock paths with $L$ trading transitions and emissions truncated as in~\eqref{eq:truncated_emission}.
\State Compute $Z^S_{j,\ell}$ by standardizing the simulated daily log returns as in~\eqref{eq:return_shock}, with constants from these paths or from a pilot simulation.
\State Draw $Z^\perp_{j,\ell}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ once per path and transition; set $Z^v_{j,\ell}\gets\rho Z^S_{j,\ell}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}Z^\perp_{j,\ell}$ and reuse it for every contract.
\For{contract $a$ and path $j$}
  \State $v_{a,0}^{(j)} \gets \theta_i\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau_0,K/S_0)$
  \For{$\ell=0$ \textbf{to} $L-1$}
    \State $\sigma_{a,\ell}^{(j)} \gets \sqrt{v_{a,\ell}^{(j)}}$
    \State $P_{a,\ell}^{(j)} \gets \mathrm{LR}_{N_{\mathrm{LR}}}(S_{j,\ell},K,\sigma_{a,\ell}^{(j)},\tau_\ell/365,r)$
    \State $\bar v_{a,\ell}^{(j)}\gets\theta_i\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tau_\ell,K/S_{j,\ell})$
    \State Update $v_{a,\ell+1}^{(j)}$ with the floored Euler step in~\eqref{eq:euler} using $\Delta t=1/252$.
  \EndFor
  \State $P_{a,L}^{(j)}\gets\max\{\phi(S_{j,L}-K),0\}$.
\EndFor
\State \Return all paths.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For a short position sold at premium $p_0$, profit and loss (P\&L) at expiration was given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:terminal_pnl}
 \Pi_T^{\mathrm{put}}=p_0^{\mathrm{put}}-\max(K-S_T,0),
 \qquad
 \Pi_T^{\mathrm{call}}=p_0^{\mathrm{call}}-\max(S_T-K,0),
\end{equation}
where $S_T$ is the JumpHMM stock price at expiration. We assumed entry at the observed midpoint, no transaction costs, no margin constraint, and no interest on the premium received. We also ignored early assignment. We valued each short position at expiration and, in the IV ablation, after five, ten, fifteen, or twenty trading days. These valuation dates were fixed across paths, even though the tree prices include the holder's right to exercise early. With early assignment ignored, the P\&L at expiration depends only on $S_T$, the strike, and the premium, so the IV updates changed option prices and Greeks before expiration but not the payoff of a position held to expiration. All premiums, option values, and P\&L are in dollars per share, so amounts for a standard 100-share contract are 100 times these values. We calculated the Greeks on the same LR tree by central finite differences, which reprice the option after equal upward and downward changes to one input. Delta measures the price's sensitivity to the stock price, gamma measures how delta changes with the stock price, and Vega measures the price's sensitivity to IV. Delta and gamma used stock-price bumps of $\pm1.5\%$. Vega used IV bumps of $\pm0.005$ in absolute terms and is reported per volatility percentage point.

\subsection{Comparison of five IV variants}\label{sec:ablation_methods}

To find which parts of the IV update changed option values before expiration, we repriced the GS and LLY options, sold at the same April 28 premiums, under five IV variants in the IV ablation. Within each seed, the five variants shared the same simulated stock paths. The first ablation seed equals the illustration seed, but the ablation set its growth shift from the pilot simulation, so its paths are not the illustration paths. Frozen IV kept each option's IV at its fitted entry value. The direct surface set the factor equal to its target, $v_{a,t}=\bar v_{a,t}$, on every date. Mean reversion, a deterministic variant, used the update in~\eqref{eq:euler} without shocks ($\sigma_v=0$). The uncoupled factor added random shocks unrelated to stock returns ($\sigma_v=0.5$, $\rho=0$). The full factor linked those shocks to stock returns ($\sigma_v=0.5$, $\rho=-0.6$). All three variants that followed~\eqref{eq:euler} used $\kappa=15$. Every variant started from the same fitted entry IV, and the two stochastic variants reused the same independent normal shocks, so results could be compared path by path. We ran the ablation with three random-number seeds per ticker (the integers 20260429, 20260430, and 20260431) and 1{,}000 paths per seed. Its pilot simulation used 10{,}000 paths (seed 20260905) and the same 22 trading-day steps. It fixed the growth shift for the 10\% target and the return mean and standard deviation before the evaluation paths were drawn.

We compared the variants by hypothetical short-position P\&L at fixed horizons. The primary horizon was ten trading days, on May 12 with 17 calendar days to expiration; secondary horizons were five, fifteen, and twenty trading days. We used no profit target, stop-loss, or rule for choosing when to close a position. We used the direct surface as the baseline, because its IV sits exactly on the target that the three updating variants move toward. The hypothetical P\&L at each horizon, and its difference from the direct surface on the same path, were given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ablation_pnl}
    \Pi_{a,t}^{(g,j)}=p_{a,0}-P_{a,t}^{(g,j)},
    \qquad D_{a,t}^{(g,j)}=\Pi_{a,t}^{(g,j)}-\Pi_{a,t}^{(\mathrm{surface},j)},
\end{equation}
where $g$ denotes the IV variant, $j$ the shared stock path, $p_{a,0}$ the entry premium, and $P_{a,t}^{(g,j)}$ the option's tree price on date $t$. Because the premium is the market midpoint while later prices come from the tree, $\Pi$ also includes the gap between the two at entry, and this gap cancels in $D$. For each variant, we reported the mean of $\Pi$, its standard deviation, its 5\% quantile, and its 5\% expected shortfall, defined as the mean P\&L at or below that quantile. The Monte Carlo standard error of the mean paired difference was $s_D/\sqrt{N_p}$, where $s_D$ is the standard deviation of $D$ across the $N_p$ paths, 1{,}000 per seed or 3{,}000 pooled. These standard errors measure simulation noise with the fitted models and pilot constants held fixed. Price differences on individual paths can cancel in the mean, so we also reported the mean absolute difference between each variant's price and the direct-surface price on the same path. We reported each seed separately and also pooled the 3{,}000 paths per ticker.

We checked that entry prices and payoffs at expiration agreed exactly across variants, as they must by construction. We also checked the numerical accuracy of the prices in between. For every variant, we compared 201-step and 401-step LR prices on the first twenty paths of every seed after ten and twenty trading days and recorded the largest absolute difference. The tree prices each option separately, so nothing forces prices to be consistent across strikes. We therefore priced calls and puts at eleven fixed strikes from 0.8 to 1.2 times the entry stock price, a check we call the strike audit. The audit ran within the first ablation seed. Each audit strike used the May 29 expiration and its own factor and target, driven by the same shocks as the GS or LLY options. The audit used the first fifty paths of the first seed at entry and after ten and twenty trading days, at both 201 and 401 steps. Entry, which all paths share, was checked once. Each ticker, variant, option type, and tree size therefore had 101 stock-price points, the entry price and fifty paths on each of two dates. At each of these points, we checked four conditions, and a violation's size was the dollar amount by which a condition failed. Each price had to lie between its intrinsic value and an upper bound, the stock price for a call and the strike for a put. As the strike increased, call prices could not rise and put prices could not fall. The price difference between adjacent strikes could not exceed the distance between the strikes (the vertical-spread bound). Prices had to be convex in strike, so no price could exceed the straight line between its neighbors. For all four checks, we counted violations larger than \$0.01 per share. Passing these checks is necessary, but not sufficient, for prices to be free of arbitrage across strikes. The audit compared strikes within one expiration on each date, so it did not test consistency across expiration dates.
