\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

We first report fitted and temporal-holdout IV errors, then use GS and LLY contracts to demonstrate the complete forward-simulation pipeline. The scenario results are fitted illustrations rather than additional validation experiments.

\subsection{Cross-sectional IV-surface performance}\label{sec:ladder}

We first asked whether added surface capacity and narrower parameter sharing reduced IV error. The corpus contained 234{,}549 observations for 31 tickers and fifteen capture dates from April 14 through May 11, 2026. We retained contracts with moneyness $K/S\in[0.80,1.20]$, positive bids, positive DTE, and reported IV between 0.01 and 2.0. The six groups were technology, healthcare, financials, energy, retail, and broad-market exchange-traded funds. Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:sector_map} gives the ticker and observation counts.

We fitted three models with the common loss in~\eqref{eq:nn_loss}: one global five-parameter shape, one global neural shape, and one neural shape per sector. All three used ticker-specific level parameters and differed only in shape capacity or sharing. On the pooled training corpus, RMSE fell from 12.48 to 11.47 volatility percentage points when the global neural surface replaced the parametric surface. Sector networks reduced it further to 10.24 points (Table~\ref{tab:three_way}). These changes corresponded to relative in-sample reductions of 8.1\% and 17.9\% from the parametric reference.

The sector comparison showed where narrower sharing helped. Sector networks improved on the global network in all six groups, with the largest absolute reductions for exchange-traded funds and financials (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:per_sector}). Technology and healthcare retained the largest residual RMSE. Their worst-fitting tickers included QCOM, MU, INTC, AMD, PFE, and MRNA (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:worst_tickers}). This pattern showed that one surface per sector did not capture all within-sector variation. It did not identify a specific economic cause for each residual.

We also held out May 11, retrained on the other fourteen dates, and standardized inputs using training rows only. Pooled train RMSE was 10.21 points and test RMSE was 10.89 points, a 0.68-point gap (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:loo_sector_nn}). Energy and retail had larger gaps than the other sectors. This single date and single training seed provide a limited temporal check. They do not establish generalization across market regimes or rule out overfitting.

\subsection{Per-ticker neural surfaces}\label{sec:per_ticker_nn}

We next asked whether data-rich tickers benefited from their own shape. Twenty-nine of the 31 tickers exceeded 2{,}000 observations and received per-ticker networks; PFE and BMY retained their sector networks. Tickers with at least 5{,}000 observations used the 337-parameter architecture, while the rest used 105 parameters. The combined model reduced pooled in-sample RMSE from 10.24 to 9.73 volatility percentage points and improved 21 of the 29 qualified tickers. JNJ, MRNA, AAPL, INTC, AMGN, MU, and NVDA had the largest reductions.

The representative panels show that the per-ticker curves followed local smile differences that were averaged away by the sector surfaces (Fig.~\ref{fig:per_ticker_panels}). These are fitted comparisons on the same pooled corpus used for training. The separate ticker levels and network shapes should not be interpreted individually because the additive output bias and log level are not identified. The fitted IV product in~\eqref{eq:sigma_model}, and its prediction error, are the meaningful quantities.

\centering

\caption{Fitted IV smiles for six representative tickers at a middle maturity. Points show market IV, the solid curve shows the per-ticker neural surface, and the dashed curve shows the sector surface. All observations came from the fifteen-date training corpus.}\label{fig:per_ticker_panels}

We translated IV errors into dollar-price errors on the May 11 capture using a 200-step CRR American tree (Fig.~\ref{fig:price_error}). The per-ticker model placed 33\% of the plotted SPY contracts and 88\% of the plotted GS contracts inside the local bid-ask half-spread band. On NVDA, all three surface tiers remained within about $\$0.70$. All model tiers overvalued the plotted LLY contracts by about $\$2$ to $\$6$, while the market-IV reference was near zero by construction. This comparison suggested that the LLY error arose mainly from a day-specific IV-level mismatch, not from the lattice implementation. The check still used the same date that contributed to the full-corpus surface fit, so it was descriptive rather than out of sample.

\subsection{Temporal holdout with earnings inputs}\label{sec:earnings_holdout}

A separate eight-day subset tested a fixed two-day temporal holdout. The training block contained six capture days from April 14 through April 22 and 103{,}897 rows. The April 23 and April 24 test block contained 26{,}833 rows. Every test ticker appeared during training. We compared a two-input pooled model (A), a two-input model after removing rows close to ticker or peer earnings dates from both splits (B), and a four-input earnings-aware model on the original rows (C).

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{Temporal-holdout comparison. RMSE and gaps are in volatility percentage points. Configuration B used different row sets from A and C.}\label{tab:earnings_holdout}
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
Configuration & $N_{\mathrm{tr}}$ & $N_{\mathrm{te}}$ & Train RMSE & Test RMSE & Gap \\
\midrule
A: pooled, two inputs          & 103{,}897 & 26{,}833 & 8.05 & 13.03 & $+4.99$ \\
B: non-earnings, two inputs    &  29{,}383 &  4{,}516 & 8.02 &  7.96 & $-0.06$ \\
C: earnings-aware, four inputs & 103{,}897 & 26{,}833 & 7.61 & 11.98 & $+4.36$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}}
\end{table}

The earnings-aware model reduced test RMSE from 13.03 to 11.98 points, an absolute reduction of 1.05 points and a relative reduction of 8.1\% (Table~\ref{tab:earnings_holdout}). Its train-to-test gap fell from 4.99 to 4.36 points, a 12.6\% reduction. The largest ticker-level improvements occurred among technology names whose peers reported near the holdout dates (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:tech_pivot}). INTC error increased, which was consistent with a calendar feature being unable to encode the realized earnings surprise.

Configuration B had nearly equal train and test RMSE, but it removed most rows and changed the composition of both samples. It therefore did not show that earnings caused all of Configuration A's gap. It only showed that the retained non-event subset was easier under this split. More dates, repeated seeds, and matched-row comparisons are needed to separate event effects from changes in ticker and contract composition.
