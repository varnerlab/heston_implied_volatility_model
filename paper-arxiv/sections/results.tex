\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

To test whether IV surfaces could serve as targets, we fitted parametric and neural surfaces to the calibration data and observed lower in-sample error as the surfaces became more flexible and were shared by fewer tickers (Table~\ref{tab:three_way}). The filtered data contained 234{,}549 observations from 31 tickers across fifteen snapshots and six market groups (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:sector_map} and~\ref{tab:corpus_dates}). Every model used the same loss and kept a separate level for each ticker. Root mean squared error (RMSE) measured agreement with reported IV in volatility percentage points. Replacing the global parametric shape with a global neural surface reduced pooled in-sample error from 12.48 to 11.47 points. Sector networks reduced it further, to 10.24 points. They improved all six groups, most in exchange-traded funds (ETFs) and financials (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:per_sector}). Technology and healthcare kept the largest errors and contained the weakest individual ticker fits (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:worst_tickers}). These remaining differences motivated separate surfaces for tickers with enough observations. Giving each such ticker its own network, while keeping sector networks for the rest, reduced pooled error to 9.73 points. This error was 22\% below that of the parametric surface, and most qualifying tickers improved (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:per_ticker_comparison}). We next asked whether the fits held on individual dates, in dollar prices, and on later dates.

We first compared the fitted surfaces with the observations on a single date, May 11, and found errors in the overall IV level that carried into option prices (Figs.~\ref{fig:per_ticker_panels} and~\ref{fig:price_error}). The surfaces had been fitted on all fifteen snapshots, including May 11, so the comparison was in-sample. Near the money, the per-ticker fits exceeded observed IV by an average of 9.45 percentage points for MSFT and 7.90 points for LLY (Fig.~\ref{fig:per_ticker_panels}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:smile_date_bias}). The biases were small for SPY and GS, whereas the fit underpredicted AVGO by 5.37 points. These errors occurred at quoted contracts, not in extrapolated parts of the surface. A surface whose only inputs are moneyness and maturity cannot shift its level for one date. To measure the effect on prices, we priced each option with its fitted IV in the American tree and compared each dollar error with the local bid--ask half-spread. We used the half-spread because the same IV error moves prices by different amounts across contracts. Only about one third of the fitted SPY, NVDA, and LLY prices fell within the half-spread around the mid, compared with 88\% for GS (Fig.~\ref{fig:price_error}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:price_error_summary}). The largest median absolute error, \$4.01 per share, was for LLY. Repeating the calculation with each contract's reported IV in the same tree cut the LLY median error six-fold, to \$0.67 per share. The surface's IV level on that date was therefore a major source of the price error. Even with the reported IV, the American tree missed some captured mids. A better pooled fit still left errors on individual dates, and dollar prices needed a check of their own.

Because the May 11 comparison was in-sample, we next withheld dates from training and observed higher errors on those dates, which earnings-calendar inputs reduced on one fixed holdout (Table~\ref{tab:earnings_holdout}). One test removed May 11 and retrained the sector networks on the other fourteen dates. Pooled error was 10.21 volatility percentage points on the training data and 10.89 points on the held-out date (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:loo_sector_nn}). Error rose most in energy and retail, so the small pooled gap hid differences among sectors. A separate temporal split then tested whether calendar inputs added information beyond moneyness and maturity. Configuration A used those two inputs alone. Configuration C added the distances to the ticker's and its peers' earnings dates, on exactly the same training and test rows. The added inputs reduced error on the two-day holdout from 13.03 to 11.98 volatility percentage points. Several technology tickers with nearby peer reports improved, although not every ticker did (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:tech_pivot}). Configuration B instead removed observations near earnings before fitting the two-input model, and its training and test errors were similar. Because it discarded most observations and changed both samples, it could not isolate earnings as the cause of the original gap. Transfer to new dates was therefore limited, and earnings inputs helped on the one holdout tested.
