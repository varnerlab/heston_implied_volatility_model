\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

We fitted global parametric, global neural, and sector-specific neural surfaces to the calibration corpus and observed lower in-sample IV error as surface flexibility increased and parameter sharing narrowed (Table~\ref{tab:three_way}). The filtered corpus contained 234{,}549 observations from 31 tickers across fifteen snapshots and six market groups (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:sector_map} and~\ref{tab:corpus_dates}), and every model used the same loss and retained ticker-specific levels. Root mean squared error (RMSE) measured agreement with reported IV in volatility percentage points, with lower values indicating a closer fit, and we compared models through their predicted IV because the ticker level and neural output bias were not separately identified in~\eqref{eq:sigma_model}. Replacing the global parametric shape with a global neural surface reduced pooled in-sample error from 12.48 to 11.47 points, and sector-specific networks reduced it further to 10.24 points. The sector gains extended across all six groups and were largest in exchange-traded funds (ETFs) and financials (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:per_sector}), whereas technology and healthcare retained the largest errors and contained the weakest individual ticker fits (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:worst_tickers}). These remaining differences motivated separate surfaces for tickers with sufficient observations. Assigning per-ticker networks where the sample threshold was met, while retaining sector fits for the remaining tickers, reduced pooled error to 9.73 points, 22\% below the parametric reference, and most qualified tickers improved under this change (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:per_ticker_comparison}). Pooled calibration error alone could not show whether the fits were accurate on individual dates, in dollar prices, or on later observations, and we examined each of these in turn.

We then compared the fitted surfaces with observations from the May 11 capture and found date-specific level errors that carried through to option prices (Figs.~\ref{fig:per_ticker_panels} and~\ref{fig:price_error}). All three displayed surfaces had been fitted across the fifteen snapshots that included this date, so the comparison was in-sample. Near the money, the per-ticker fits exceeded observed IV by an average of 9.45 percentage points for MSFT and 7.90 points for LLY, whereas the corresponding biases were small for SPY and GS and AVGO was underpredicted by 5.37 points (Fig.~\ref{fig:per_ticker_panels}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:smile_date_bias}). These discrepancies appeared at the observed contracts themselves, and a surface with moneyness and maturity as its only inputs could not adjust its level independently for a single date. To measure the pricing consequences, we passed the fitted IV predictions to the American lattice and judged each dollar error against the local bid--ask half-spread, since the same IV error can have different pricing consequences across contracts and an improvement in pooled IV fit need not place a model price within the quoted spread. Only about one third of fitted SPY, NVDA, and LLY prices fell within the half-spread around the mid, compared with 88\% for GS (Fig.~\ref{fig:price_error}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:price_error_summary}), and LLY had the largest median absolute error at \$4.01 per share. Repeating the calculation with each contract's reported IV in the same lattice cut the LLY median error six-fold, to \$0.67 per share, which pointed to the surface's date-specific IV level as a major contributor. The remaining errors also showed that the reported IV did not reproduce every captured mid under the American pricer. Greater flexibility and narrower sharing therefore improved the pooled fit without removing date-level errors, which made dollar-price accuracy a separate requirement alongside IV fit.

We next withheld dates from training and observed higher errors on the withheld dates, which earnings-calendar inputs reduced on a fixed holdout (Table~\ref{tab:earnings_holdout}). Removing May 11 and retraining the sector models on the other fourteen dates gave pooled error of 10.21 volatility percentage points on the training observations and 10.89 points on the holdout, with the largest increases in energy and retail, so the modest pooled gap concealed differences among sectors (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:loo_sector_nn}). A separate temporal split then tested whether calendar inputs supplied information beyond moneyness and maturity. Configuration A used those two inputs alone, while Configuration C added distances to ticker and peer earnings dates on exactly the same training and test rows. The added inputs reduced error on the two-day holdout from 13.03 to 11.98 volatility percentage points, and several technology tickers with nearby peer reports improved, although the gains did not extend to every ticker (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:tech_pivot}). Configuration B removed observations near earnings before fitting the two-input model and produced similar training and test errors, but because it discarded most observations and changed the composition of both samples, it could not attribute the original error gap to earnings alone. Every test ticker appeared in training, and neither temporal experiment covered repeated dates and training seeds, so these results supported limited transfer within the available window and a benefit from earnings inputs on one fixed holdout.
