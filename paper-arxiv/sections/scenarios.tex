The GS and LLY illustrations showed how contract-specific IV targets changed option values along fixed stock paths (Figs.~\ref{fig:gs_paths} and~\ref{fig:gs_iv}; Supplementary Figs.~\ref{fig:lly_paths} and~\ref{fig:lly_iv}). Each ticker used one put and one call from April 28, expiring May 29, and 1{,}000 paths with the shared settings in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:scenario_settings}. On the lowest-terminal-price GS paths, both median IVs rose as the stock fell. The call's higher strike gave it higher moneyness and a higher target on the surface's rising right wing, so its IV rose more. Part of the late rise occurred beyond the fitted moneyness limit of 1.2. Targets also rose as expiration approached, lifting both IVs near the end. Near expiration, delta approached $\pm1$ on paths heading toward exercise, gamma concentrated near the strike, and Vega declined (Supplementary Figs.~\ref{fig:gs_greeks} and~\ref{fig:lly_greeks}). These were illustrations, not forecasts, because the surfaces included later quotes. At expiration, GS short puts and calls retained their full premiums on 74.0\% and 67.7\% of paths, but the worst 5\% lost at least \$58.96 and \$86.18 per share. For LLY, the corresponding premium-retention frequencies were 80.7\% and 79.3\% (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:gs_scenario} and~\ref{tab:lly_scenario}). With early assignment ignored, these terminal outcomes depended on stock prices, strikes, and premiums, not the IV update. Its effect therefore had to be measured before expiration.
