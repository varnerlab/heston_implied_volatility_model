Having evaluated the fitted surfaces, we used them to simulate complete GS contract paths and observed option values that followed the stock path and IV factors that differed between the put and call (Figs.~\ref{fig:gs_paths} and~\ref{fig:gs_iv}). We selected one put and one call for each of GS and LLY from the April 28 capture with expiration on May 29 (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:gs_scenario} and~\ref{tab:lly_scenario}). The initial illustrations used 1{,}000 stock paths per ticker and a separate IV factor for each contract under the fixed settings in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:scenario_settings}, with each factor following a moving target from the fitted surface before its IV entered the lattice at the current stock price and remaining maturity. The GS paths showed the expected inverse relation between terminal stock and put payoff and the direct relation between terminal stock and call payoff (Fig.~\ref{fig:gs_paths}). Before expiration, the put and call factors followed different targets as moneyness and calendar days to expiration changed, and their shared negative return coupling tended to increase factor shocks after lower standardized returns (Fig.~\ref{fig:gs_iv}). The LLY paths applied the same calculation under a different return marginal and a higher fitted IV level (Supplementary Figs.~\ref{fig:lly_paths} and~\ref{fig:lly_iv}), and the pathwise Greeks for both tickers showed delta approaching the exercise states, gamma concentrating near the strike, and Vega declining toward expiration (Supplementary Figs.~\ref{fig:gs_greeks} and~\ref{fig:lly_greeks}). These illustrations standardized return shocks across the completed ensemble (Supplementary Algorithm~\ref{alg:forward_sim}), whereas the paired ablation used independent pilot constants to measure the contribution of IV dynamics on shared evaluation paths. Both experiments used surfaces fitted with observations from the scenario period, so they illustrated the behavior of the assembled model rather than forecasting later observations, which the chronological tests evaluated with separate fits and origins.

We next held each short position to expiration and observed frequent premium retention alongside lower-tail losses (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:gs_scenario} and~\ref{tab:lly_scenario}). For a position held to expiration, terminal P\&L was the entry premium minus the payoff determined by the final stock price and strike. In GS, the short put retained its full premium on 74.0\% of paths and the short call on 67.7\%, yet the worst 5\% of outcomes lost at least \$58.96 per share for the put and \$86.18 for the call, 3.6 and 5.4 times their entry premiums. Those less frequent losses left mean P\&L positive for the put but negative for the call. The market-delta heuristic $1-|\Delta|$ gave premium-retention estimates of 70.5\% and 67.2\% for the same contracts. The LLY put and call retained their premiums on 80.7\% and 79.3\% of paths, respectively, both with positive mean P\&L under the scenario assumptions, and these frequencies exceeded the heuristic by 11.0 and 10.2 percentage points. The larger gaps than in GS showed that agreement between the heuristic and simulated strike crossing depended on the ticker and scenario, since quote delta came from a risk-neutral pricing calculation whereas the simulated frequencies came from physical stock paths. Because terminal P\&L depended only on the stock path, strike, and premium, these results characterized the chosen return distributions and contract inputs and did not test the fitted surface or the dynamic factor, which required changing the IV module while holding the stock paths and terminal payoffs fixed.
