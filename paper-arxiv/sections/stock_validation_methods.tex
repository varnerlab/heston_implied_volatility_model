\subsection{Stock forecast benchmarks}\label{sec:stock_validation_methods}

After the forecast test, we evaluated JumpHMM's stock forecasts on their own, with new 10{,}000-path runs separate from the forecast test's 1{,}000 paths, because its stock forecasts had missed by more than expected. We first ruled out a coding mistake in how the paths were built. We confirmed that the simulated growth rates were converted to daily log returns with the $1/252$ step and compounded into prices once per day. We also rebuilt the prices from the saved growth rates and compared them with the simulated prices. Because the starting hidden state did not use recent returns, we next repeated the 2026 stock forecasts with the starting state drawn from the distribution that a forward filter inferred from all available past daily returns. The filter kept the fitted parameters, tracked both the hidden state and the days remaining in any jump episode in progress, and used the likelihoods of the truncated emissions in~\eqref{eq:truncated_emission}.

We then asked whether other stock forecast methods could make smaller errors without using future information. We compared four methods on equal terms, JumpHMM and the three described next. The JumpHMM forecasts started from the stationary distribution and used the pilot growth shift. The unchanged-price benchmark forecast the origin price itself and gave no uncertainty interval. The forecast test's random walk and the filtered starting state served only as diagnostics and were not part of this comparison. The adaptive-volatility model drew Gaussian log returns with zero drift and updated its daily variance with an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) of squared log returns~\cite{riskmetrics1996}. The direction-predicting model kept that variance and added a daily drift predicted by a regression. The two models were given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stock_benchmark}
a_t=\lambda a_{t-1}+(1-\lambda)u_t^2,
\qquad
\log\frac{S_{t+h}}{S_t}=h\mu_t+\sqrt{a_t}\sum_{j=1}^{h}Z_j,
\quad Z_j\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
\end{equation}
where $u_t=\log(S_t/S_{t-1})$ is the observed daily log return, $a_t$ is the estimated daily variance at origin $t$, $\lambda$ is the EWMA decay, $h$ is the horizon in trading days, and $\mu_t$ is the daily drift. The adaptive-volatility model set $\mu_t=0$, and the direction-predicting model used the regression prediction, so the adaptive-volatility model is the direction-predicting model with zero drift. The regression predicted the next day's log return divided by the EWMA volatility at the origin, and the drift $\mu_t$ was this prediction multiplied by that volatility. Its inputs were the stock's cumulative log returns over its latest one, five, and twenty daily returns, and the cumulative log return of SPY over its latest five daily returns. The fund SPY tracks the S\&P 500. Each input was divided by the square root of its window length and by its own asset's EWMA volatility at the origin, so SPY's input used SPY's volatility. We fitted the regression separately for GS and LLY, without an intercept, and a ridge penalty shrank its coefficients toward zero~\cite{hoerl1970}. Drift and variance stayed fixed within each forecast horizon, so neither model used future stock or market returns. Neither model used the return cutoff, which applies only to JumpHMM. These stock tests did not change the paths used in the option forecasts or the IV ablation.

We selected the settings of the adaptive-volatility and direction-predicting models on annual validation blocks from 2019 through 2024, using only data before each block to fit the regression and its input scaling. After the window and volatility scaling, each input column was also divided by its root mean square in the training data. We chose one EWMA decay and one ridge penalty for both tickers, weighting each ticker, validation year, and horizon (one and five sessions) equally. Candidate decays were $0.80$, $0.90$, $0.94$, $0.97$, and $0.99$, and we chose the decay that minimized the Gaussian log loss of cumulative log returns. Candidate ridge penalties ranged from $0.01$ to $1000$ and included the zero-drift limit, an infinite penalty that sets every coefficient to zero. We chose the penalty that minimized the squared cumulative-return error divided by the forecast variance. After selection, we fitted the regression on 2014--2024 observations and froze its coefficients and input scaling.

We then tested all four methods in two later periods. The daily closes for 2014 through 2025 came from the S\&P 500 daily price files included with the code, and the 2026 closes came from the closing prices stored in the option files. Because the 2026 closes begin in April, the EWMA variance restarted in April from its 2014--2024 estimate. The 2025 test supplied 249 one-session and 245 five-session origins per ticker. The August--September 2026 test used the option-test origins with an observed stock price at the endpoint, 21 at one session and 17 at five sessions per ticker. All methods used identical starting prices and endpoints. Features and variance at each origin used only data available at that time. A missing daily close was left as a gap, so every return spanned exactly one trading day. Each stochastic method used 10{,}000 paths per origin for each of three fixed seeds. The adaptive-volatility and direction-predicting models shared the same Gaussian shocks. We reported the MAE of the median forecast and the RMSE of the mean forecast, because the median minimizes expected absolute error and the mean minimizes expected squared error~\cite{hyndman2021}. The option forecast test, by contrast, fixed MAE of the mean in advance as its primary score. For the three methods with forecast distributions, we also reported CRPS and the coverage and width of central 90\% intervals. Scores were averaged across dates within each simulation seed and then across seeds. We proposed the adaptive-volatility and direction-predicting models after seeing JumpHMM's August and September 2026 stock-forecast errors, although their settings came from the 2019--2024 validation blocks. We therefore reported the two periods separately and treated the 2026 comparison as exploratory.
