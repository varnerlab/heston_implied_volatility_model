\section{Forward-simulated short-premium scenarios}\label{sec:scenarios}

To exercise the calibrated framework as a synthetic-data generator and stress test, we forward-simulated 1{,}000 LLY price paths over a 31 trading-day horizon and tracked the path-conditional fair value of two real LLY option contracts pulled directly from the 2026-04-28 ladder capture: a short put at $K_p = \$825$ (market $\Delta = -0.303$, mid \$23.30, market IV $44.4\%$) and a short call at $K_c = \$940$ (market $\Delta = +0.309$, mid \$20.76, market IV $44.0\%$), both expiring 2026-05-29 with the underlying spot at $S_0 = \$873.83$. Each path drew from the JumpHMM marginal pretrained on LLY returns. Conditional on the path, we evolved a Cox--Ingersoll--Ross variance level $v_t$ with mean-reversion $\kappa = 2.0$, vol-of-vol $\sigma_v = 0.5$, and leverage coupling $\rho = -0.6$, and at every trading day along every path we re-priced both contracts via 400-step CRR American with $\sigma_t^2 = v_t \cdot \psi_{\mathrm{NN}}((K/S_t)_{\mathrm{std}}, (T-t)_{\mathrm{std}})$, the per-ticker neural smile/term-structure surface from \S\ref{sec:ladder}. The seller received the market mid as the entry premium, and the model marked the position to its CRR American fair value at every subsequent step; we assumed no scheduled earnings event during the 31-day window so the only sources of variation were diffusion, jumps, and the variance dynamics themselves. The model's $t=0$ fair value sat slightly above the market mid on both legs (put: \$25.91 vs.\ \$23.30, edge $+\$2.61$; call: \$21.34 vs.\ \$20.76, edge $+\$0.58$), so the simulated short was selling at a small discount to the model and would have realised that entry edge on top of the path-conditional terminal P\&L.

Figures~\ref{fig:lly_put_paths} and~\ref{fig:lly_call_paths} render the share-price ensemble alongside the short-put and short-call premium ensembles. On the worst-5\% paths (red, Fig.~\ref{fig:lly_put_paths}), LLY drifted toward and through $K_p$, the short-put premium more than tripled to roughly \$80, and the position closed with a mean P\&L of $-\$58.01$ on those paths. On the top 1--5\% paths (green, Fig.~\ref{fig:lly_call_paths}; the wildest 1\% are excluded for legibility), LLY pushed past $K_c$ and the short-call premium tripled despite the calendar pulling extrinsic value toward zero; mean P\&L on the top-5\% paths was $-\$142.81$, more than $2.4{\times}$ the mean loss on the worst put paths. On both panels the median path stayed inside the 25--75\% band and closed flat at the premium received --- the short-premium trade made money on the typical path, with both legs carrying a long left tail.

The IV trajectories in Figure~\ref{fig:lly_iv} make explicit why the short-call leg lost asymmetrically. Both panels showed the implied volatility at the contract strike rising as $t \to T$, but the rise was not a model artefact: it is the empirical short-dated wing-bow that the per-ticker $\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}$ learned from the ladder corpus. As $(T-t)$ shrank at fixed $\log(K/S)$, the standardised moneyness drifted deeper into the wing where $\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}$ is monotonically larger --- a Gaussian diffusion would underprice that wing without the inflation, since short-horizon returns are far more leptokurtic than the central-limit-theorem regime of long-horizon returns. The level $v_t$ contributed only a slow mean-reverting drift around $\bar\theta$, so the rise was essentially a smile-shape effect at a fixed point on the surface. On the green tail $S_t$ pushed away from the call strike, dragging the standardised moneyness even further into the wing and amplifying the rise; on the red tail the leverage coupling lifted $v_t$ at the same time as $S_t$ fell, compounding the IV climb.

Figure~\ref{fig:lly_greeks} decomposes the position risk into $\Delta$, $\Gamma$, and Vega along the same paths, computed by central finite differences on the same 400-step CRR American pricer used for the base marks (spot bumps of $\pm 1.5\%$ for $\Delta$ and $\Gamma$; $\sigma$ bumps of $\pm 0.005$ for Vega-per-1\%-IV). The long-put $\Delta$ began near $-0.30$ and bifurcated: on the worst-5\% paths it ran to $-1$ as the put pinned ITM at expiry, while on the rest it ran to $0$; the long-call mirror image held at the call strike on the top tail. $\Gamma$ collapsed to zero on every path that drifted away from its strike but spiked sharply on paths that pinned to the strike near expiry, exhibiting the canonical short-gamma blowup geometry that motivates the practitioner aversion to short-premium expiry-day exposure. Vega decayed monotonically toward zero on every path --- the $\sqrt{T-t}$ factor dominates --- but the worst-5\% put paths and top-5\% call paths held materially more vega than the median through about $t = 20$, which exposed the short-premium trader to vol-of-vol risk on precisely the paths that were already losing on $\Delta$. The signs of the position-level Greeks for the short trader are the negatives of the long Greeks shown.

Figure~\ref{fig:lly_pnl} summarises the terminal P\&L distributions and Table~\ref{tab:lly_scenario} reports the moments and tail statistics. Both legs showed the characteristic short-premium asymmetry: a thin probability mass piled at the premium ceiling, a long left tail. The short put kept its full \$23.30 premium on $83.1\%$ of paths and closed with a mean P\&L of $+\$16.94$; the short call kept its full \$20.76 premium on $70.2\%$ of paths but its mean P\&L was essentially zero ($+\$1.86$) and the worst-case loss reached $-\$325.62$, $1.87{\times}$ the worst put loss and $15.7{\times}$ the premium itself. The asymmetry between the two legs reflected the negative skew that LLY's per-ticker $\psi_{\mathrm{NN}}$ encodes (higher IV at down-strikes than at equally-far up-strikes) together with the leverage coupling that lifted $v_t$ on down-paths --- the put leg was implicitly priced for the down-tail and earned a fair premium for it, while the call leg encountered a fatter empirical right-tail than its strike-symmetric BS-implied premium accounted for.
